Raw figures from financial statements are hard to compare: is GH₵ 24 million of profit good? It depends on how big the company is, what it had to invest, how much it borrowed, and what last year and its rivals looked like. **Financial ratios** divide one figure by another to answer those questions. By the end of this chapter you will be able to work out the main ratios in five families (profitability, efficiency, liquidity, debt, and per-share and market ratios), interpret them, break return on equity into its parts, and avoid the traps that make ratios mislead. The examples use Volta Foods PLC from <Ref to="fi-company-statements" />; all figures are fictional and in GH₵ million unless stated. This is general education, not advice on any company.

## Profitability: how much of each cedi is kept

- **Gross margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue = 105 ÷ 300 = **35.0%** (2025: 33.8%).
- **Operating margin** = operating profit ÷ revenue = 45 ÷ 300 = **15.0%** (2025: 13.8%).
- **Net margin** = profit ÷ revenue = 24 ÷ 300 = **8.0%** (2025: 6.9%).

All three rose. Volta kept 35 pesewas of each cedi of sales after the direct cost of making its food, 15 after running the business, and 8 after interest and tax.

- **Return on equity** (ROE) = profit ÷ equity = 24 ÷ 109.6 = **21.9%** (2025: 18.0%): the return on the shareholders’ money in the business.
- **Return on assets** (ROA) = profit ÷ total assets = 24 ÷ 229.6 = **10.5%** (2025: 8.1%): how well all the assets, however financed, produce profit.

Some analysts divide by the average of the opening and closing figures instead of the year end: Volta’s ROE on average equity is 24 ÷ 104.8 = 22.9%. Either is fine, as long as you use the same method for every year and company you compare.

## Efficiency: how hard the assets work

- **Asset turnover** = revenue ÷ total assets = 300 ÷ 229.6 = **1.31** (2025: 1.17): GH₵ 1.31 of sales a year for each cedi of assets.
- **Inventory days** = inventories ÷ cost of sales × 365 = 40 ÷ 195 × 365 = **74.9 days**: how long food sits as stock before being sold.
- **Receivable days** = trade receivables ÷ revenue × 365 = 35 ÷ 300 × 365 = **42.6 days**: how long customers take to pay.
- **Payable days** = trade payables ÷ cost of sales × 365 = 30 ÷ 195 × 365 = **56.2 days**: how long Volta takes to pay suppliers.

Together they give the **cash conversion cycle**: 74.9 + 42.6 − 56.2 = **61.3 days**, how long cash is tied up between paying suppliers and being paid by customers. In 2025 it was 57.0 days, mainly because Volta now pays suppliers faster (56.2 days against 59.4). A longer cycle needs more cash to run the same business.

## Liquidity: can it pay its bills?

- **Current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities = (40 + 35 + 4.6) ÷ 30 = **2.65** (2025: 2.96).
- **Quick ratio** = (current assets − inventories) ÷ current liabilities = (35 + 4.6) ÷ 30 = **1.32** (2025: 1.71). Inventory is left out because it may not turn into cash quickly.

Above 1 means short-term assets cover short-term debts. Volta is comfortable, but its quick ratio fell because its cash fell from 18.0 to 4.6.

## Debt: how much risk the borrowing adds

- **Debt to equity** = borrowings ÷ equity = 90 ÷ 109.6 = **0.82** (2025: 0.95). (Some versions use all liabilities, as in the SEC’s guide; say which you use.)
- **Net debt** = borrowings − cash = 90 − 4.6 = **85.4**.
- **Net debt to EBITDA** = 85.4 ÷ 60 = **1.42 years** (2025: 1.57). **EBITDA** is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: operating profit + depreciation = 45 + 15 = 60, a rough measure of the cash the operations produce. The ratio says roughly how many years of that it would take to repay the debt.
- **Interest cover** = operating profit ÷ finance costs = 45 ÷ 13 = **3.46 times** (2025: 3.0). Below about 2, a fall in profit could leave a company unable to pay its interest.

Debt magnifies returns to shareholders, as leverage did for a house in <Ref to="fi-property" />, and magnifies risk.

## Per-share and market ratios

These combine the accounts with the share price of GH₵ 4.05 (<Ref to="fi-shares" />):

- **Earnings per share** = 24 ÷ 120 = **GH₵ 0.20** (2025: 0.15), up 33.3%.
- **Price to earnings** = 4.05 ÷ 0.20 = **20.25**. Its inverse, the **earnings yield**, is 0.20 ÷ 4.05 = **4.94%**.
- **Dividend yield** = 0.12 ÷ 4.05 = **2.96%**; **payout ratio** = 0.12 ÷ 0.20 = 60%; **dividend cover** = 0.20 ÷ 0.12 = **1.67 times**.
- **Book value per share** = equity ÷ shares = 109.6 ÷ 120 = GH₵ 0.91; **price to book** = 4.05 ÷ 0.91 = **4.43**. Investors pay more than four times what the accounts say the company’s net assets are worth, because they value its future profits, not its machines.
- **Enterprise value** (EV) = market capitalisation + net debt = 486 + 85.4 = **571.4**: what buying the whole business, debts included, would cost. **EV to EBITDA** = 571.4 ÷ 60 = **9.52**. Unlike P/E, it is not distorted by how much a company has borrowed, so it is useful for comparing companies with different amounts of debt.

An earnings yield of 4.94% compares with a 364-day Treasury bill at 9.45% (<Ref to="fi-cash-bonds" />). Investors buying at this price are accepting a much lower return today in the hope that profits keep growing fast. <Ref to="fi-valuation" /> asks whether that is reasonable.

## Breaking down return on equity

ROE can be split into three ratios you already have, a breakdown known as the **DuPont analysis**:

ROE = net margin × asset turnover × (total assets ÷ equity)

For Volta: 8.0% × 1.31 × 2.09 = **21.9%**. The last factor, the **equity multiplier**, measures leverage: assets are 2.09 times equity, because borrowing funds the rest.

This tells you *why* ROE is high. Volta’s ROE rose from 18.0% to 21.9% because its margin rose (6.9% to 8.0%) and its assets worked harder (1.17 to 1.31), even though it used slightly less debt (2.23 to 2.09). A rise in ROE that came only from more borrowing would be far less impressive.

The same ratios in Python (optional):

```python
y2026 = dict(revenue=300, profit=24.0, assets=229.6, equity=109.6, op_profit=45, interest=13)
y2025 = dict(revenue=260, profit=18.0, assets=223.0, equity=100.0, op_profit=36, interest=12)
for year, d in (("2026", y2026), ("2025", y2025)):
    margin = d["profit"] / d["revenue"]
    turnover = d["revenue"] / d["assets"]
    multiplier = d["assets"] / d["equity"]
    print(year, f"{margin:.1%}", f"{turnover:.2f}", f"{multiplier:.2f}",
          f"{margin * turnover * multiplier:.1%}", f"{d['op_profit'] / d['interest']:.2f}")
```

```text
2026 8.0% 1.31 2.09 21.9% 3.46
2025 6.9% 1.17 2.23 18.0% 3.00
```

## Using ratios well

- **Compare like with like.** A supermarket has thin margins and high turnover; a software company the reverse. Compare a company with its own past and with others in the same industry.
- **Look at the trend**, over three to five years, not one year.
- **Ask why.** A ratio raises questions; the notes and the business answer them.
- **Watch for one-offs**: a property sale can lift one year’s profit and every ratio built on it.
- **Check the definitions.** Companies and websites calculate “debt”, “EBITDA” or “adjusted profit” differently. Work them out yourself from the statements when it matters.
- **Remember what ratios cannot see**: the quality of management, competition, and the future.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing ratios across very different industries.**
- **Judging on one year**, especially one with large one-off items.
- **Using a company’s own “adjusted” figures** without checking what was adjusted.
- **Ignoring debt** when admiring a high ROE.
- **Reading a high dividend yield as a bargain** when it may signal an expected cut.
- **Forgetting that a low P/E can be deserved**, for a company whose profits are falling.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A company has revenue of GH₵ 80 million and operating profit of GH₵ 12 million. What is its operating margin?

<Solution>

12 ÷ 80 = **15%**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Current assets are GH₵ 50 million, of which inventories are 20; current liabilities are 25. What are the current and quick ratios?

<Solution>

Current: 50 ÷ 25 = **2.0**. Quick: (50 − 20) ÷ 25 = **1.2**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Work out Volta’s 2025 receivable days and inventory days.

<Solution>

Receivables: 30 ÷ 260 × 365 = **42.1 days**. Inventories: 35 ÷ 172 × 365 = **74.3 days**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A company has operating profit of GH₵ 20 million and pays GH₵ 16 million of interest. What is its interest cover, and why might a lender worry?

<Solution>

20 ÷ 16 = **1.25 times**. A fall of just 20% in operating profit would leave it unable to cover its interest from operations.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Company A has ROE of 25% with an equity multiplier of 5; Company B has ROE of 18% with an equity multiplier of 1.5. Which return is of higher quality, and why?

<Solution>

**B’s**. A’s high ROE comes largely from leverage (assets five times equity), which magnifies losses too. B earns a strong return with little borrowing: its margin × turnover is 18% ÷ 1.5 = 12%, against A’s 25% ÷ 5 = 5%.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** A share is priced at GH₵ 2.50 with EPS of GH₵ 0.50 and a dividend of GH₵ 0.20. Find its P/E, earnings yield, dividend yield and dividend cover.

<Solution>

P/E = 2.50 ÷ 0.50 = **5**. Earnings yield = 0.50 ÷ 2.50 = **20%**. Dividend yield = 0.20 ÷ 2.50 = **8%**. Cover = 0.50 ÷ 0.20 = **2.5 times**.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Volta’s cash conversion cycle lengthened from 57.0 to 61.3 days while revenue rose 15.4%. Explain in a sentence or two why that matters for its cash.

<Solution>

A longer cycle means each cedi of sales ties up cash for longer, and a bigger business ties up more cedis. Together they mean more cash is locked in inventory and receivables, part of why Volta’s cash fell even though profit rose.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Two food companies have P/E ratios of 20 and 8. Give two reasons the second might be cheap for a good reason, and one reason it might be a bargain.

<Solution>

Cheap for a reason: its profits may be **expected to fall** (losing customers, rising costs); it may carry **much more debt**, making its shares riskier; its profits may include a **one-off gain**. A bargain: investors may be **overreacting** to temporary bad news, and its long-term profits may be steadier than the price suggests. The ratio starts the investigation; it does not finish it.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Profitability**: gross, operating and **net margin**; **ROE** and **ROA**.
- **Efficiency**: asset turnover; inventory, receivable and payable days; the **cash conversion cycle**.
- **Liquidity**: **current** and **quick** ratios.
- **Debt**: debt to equity, **net debt**, net debt to **EBITDA**, **interest cover**.
- **Market**: EPS, **P/E** and earnings yield, dividend yield, payout and cover, **price to book**, **enterprise value** and EV to EBITDA.
- **DuPont**: ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier, showing whether returns come from profitability, efficiency or debt.
- Compare like with like, over several years, and ask why.
