Sooner or later someone will offer you an investment: a bank, a colleague, an advert, a relative, a stranger online. This chapter turns the whole course into ten questions you can ask of any offer, and then applies them to four realistic ones, from the plainly sound to the plainly fraudulent. By the end you will be able to take an unfamiliar investment, work out what it really is, who stands behind it, where its return comes from, what could go wrong, what it costs, how you get out, how it is taxed and whether it fits your plan, and decide calmly. The four offers are fictional; this is general education, not advice about any real product.

## The ten questions

1. **What exactly is it, and what do I own?** A loan, a share in a business, a unit in a fund, a plot of land, a contract, a promise? If you cannot describe it in two sentences, stop.
2. **Who is offering it, and are they licensed for it?** Check the regulator’s register yourself (<Ref to="fi-scams" />). Who holds your money or investment: an independent custodian or trustee, or the seller?
3. **Where does the return come from?** Interest from a borrower, profits from a business, rent from a tenant, a price rise. If the only explanation is new investors’ money or “our secret strategy”, walk away.
4. **What return is expected, and how does it compare?** Compare with a Treasury bill (<Ref to="fi-cash-bonds" />). A return far above it must come with far more risk (<Ref to="fi-risk-return" />). A guaranteed high return is a contradiction.
5. **What could go wrong, and what is the worst case?** Could you lose everything? Is it diversified or a single bet?
6. **What does it cost, in total?** All fees, every year and on entry and exit, in cedis (<Ref to="fi-fees" />).
7. **How easily can I get my money out?** How quickly, at what price, with what penalty?
8. **How is it taxed?** Compare returns after tax (<Ref to="fi-tax" />).
9. **Does it fit my plan?** Is my emergency fund in place, is expensive debt paid off, does its time horizon match my goal, and does it diversify what I already own (<Ref to="fi-diversification" />)?
10. **How much, if any?** Even a good investment should be a sensible share of your money; a speculative one only what you could lose entirely.

And one more test of your own state of mind: **am I being rushed, flattered or frightened?** (<Ref to="fi-behaviour" />) If so, wait.

## Offer A: a Treasury bill

Ama’s bank offers her a **364-day Treasury bill** at an interest rate of about 9.45%.

1. **What**: a short loan to the government of Ghana, repaid at face value in a year.
2. **Who**: issued by the government through the Bank of Ghana, bought through a licensed bank; held in her name at the depository.
3. **Return from**: the government pays interest, out of taxes and borrowing.
4. **Comparison**: this *is* the comparison point for everything else. With inflation at 5.5%, about 3.7% a year in real terms.
5. **Worst case**: the government cannot pay on time, as with the 2023 debt exchange of bonds (<Ref to="fi-cash-bonds" />). For a one-year bill this risk is low, but not zero.
6. **Costs**: usually none beyond any bank charge; check.
7. **Getting out**: held to maturity, a fixed date; selling early may be possible but at the market price.
8. **Tax**: interest on Treasury bills is exempt for individuals in Ghana.
9. **Fit**: suits money needed in a year or two, or the safe part of a portfolio. Too cautious for retirement money 30 years away.
10. **How much**: as much of her short-term and safe money as she likes, after an emergency fund in an instant-access account.

**Verdict: sound**, for the right purpose.

## Offer B: a share fund

Kofi is offered a **unit trust investing in Ghanaian shares**, by a fund manager on the SEC’s register.

1. **What**: units in a pooled fund of about 25 listed Ghanaian companies (<Ref to="fi-funds" />).
2. **Who**: a licensed fund manager; assets held by an independent custodian, both on the SEC register.
3. **Return from**: dividends and changes in the companies’ share prices.
4. **Comparison**: its factsheet shows 14% a year over five years, against 17% a year for its benchmark index; it trails by 3 points a year. Expected returns from shares are higher than bills, with much more volatility.
5. **Worst case**: Ghanaian shares can fall 30% or more and take years to recover; all its holdings are in one country and currency.
6. **Costs**: an ongoing charge of 2.5% a year, no entry fee. On GH₵ 20,000 that is GH₵ 500 a year, a large share of the expected real return.
7. **Getting out**: units can be sold on any working day at the NAV, paid within a few days.
8. **Tax**: dividends from Ghanaian shares carry the 8% withholding tax inside the fund; check how gains are treated.
9. **Fit**: suits money for ten years or more, once he has an emergency fund and no expensive debt; adds share exposure to his savings.
10. **How much**: part of his long-term money, in line with his asset allocation.

**Verdict: legitimate, but expensive and trailing its benchmark.** Worth comparing with cheaper funds, and with diversifying beyond Ghana if he can.

## Offer C: a farming “investment”

A colleague invites Esi to **GreenHarvest Agro**: pay GH₵ 5,000 and receive GH₵ 6,500 in six months, “guaranteed, from our maize farms”. She gets a 10% bonus for every friend she brings in. Payment is to a mobile money number.

1. **What**: unclear: not a share, not a registered loan, no documents beyond a WhatsApp flyer.
2. **Who**: not on the SEC register; money goes to a personal mobile money account.
3. **Return from**: “maize farms”, but 30% in six months is 1.30² − 1 = **69% a year**, far more than any farm earns reliably; and the recruitment bonus means part of every payout comes from new members.
4. **Comparison**: over seven times the Treasury bill rate, “guaranteed”. Impossible without huge risk.
5. **Worst case**: total loss; it has the marks of a Ponzi or pyramid scheme.
6. **Costs**: unknown.
7. **Getting out**: only if the organisers choose to pay.
8. **Tax**: unknown.
9. **Fit**: none.
10. **How much**: nothing.

**Verdict: almost certainly a fraud.** At least seven warning signs from <Ref to="fi-scams" />: guaranteed high return, recruitment bonus, no licence, personal payment account, contact through a social network, no documents, pressure to join while “places last”.

## Offer D: land in instalments

A developer offers Kwame a **plot of land** for GH₵ 120,000, paid as 24 monthly instalments of GH₵ 5,000. The title is “being processed”. Instalments are not refunded if he stops paying.

1. **What**: a promise to transfer a plot of land once all instalments are paid and the title is ready.
2. **Who**: a private developer; land sales are not licensed like investments, so check the company’s registration, its history, and above all the land’s title at the Lands Commission, with a lawyer (<Ref to="fi-property" />).
3. **Return from**: any future rise in the land’s value, or use of it to build. Land produces no income until something is built.
4. **Comparison**: no stated return. Is GH₵ 120,000 a fair price? Compare with recent sales nearby.
5. **Worst case**: the land has a disputed or false title, or is sold to others too, and the money is lost; or Kwame cannot keep paying and forfeits everything paid.
6. **Costs**: legal fees, registration, survey, and the opportunity cost of GH₵ 120,000 tied up (at 9.45%, about GH₵ 11,340 a year).
7. **Getting out**: very hard; land is slow to sell and cannot be sold in parts. Before full payment, he may not be able to sell at all.
8. **Tax**: check stamp duty and registration fees; any gain on a later sale may be taxable.
9. **Fit**: a large, single, illiquid asset. Reasonable only if he plans to build on it, has an emergency fund, and the commitment does not crowd out other priorities.
10. **How much**: a big share of his wealth in one place: concentration risk.

**Verdict: possibly legitimate, but high-risk until the title is proved.** Do not pay before an independent title search; insist on stage payments tied to documents; get a written contract that sets out refunds.

## Using the checklist

- **Write the answers down.** Gaps in your answers are information.
- **Get documents**: the licence, the terms, the factsheet, the contract. Legitimate providers have them.
- **Take your time.** A good investment will still be available next week.
- **Ask someone independent**, who earns nothing if you buy.
- **Prefer simple to complicated.** Complexity usually hides costs or risks.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from the return**, not from what the investment is.
- **Trusting the messenger** (a friend, a pastor, a famous face) instead of checking the firm.
- **Ignoring how to get out** until you need to.
- **Comparing with nothing**: a return only means something against a Treasury bill or a benchmark.
- **Putting too much in one place**, however good it looks.
- **Deciding under pressure.**

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** An offer promises 25% in three months. What is that over a year, compounded?

<Solution>

1.25⁴ − 1 = **144%** a year.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Which of the ten questions would most quickly expose Offer C?

<Solution>

Question 2 (**licensed?** It is not on the SEC register and wants payment to a personal account) or question 3 (**where does the return come from?** Recruitment bonuses and an impossible guaranteed return).

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Offer B trails its benchmark by 3 points a year. Over 10 years, on GH₵ 20,000, what does that cost if the benchmark returns 17% a year and the fund 14%?

<Solution>

Benchmark: 20,000 × 1.17¹⁰ = GH₵ 96,136.57. Fund: 20,000 × 1.14¹⁰ = GH₵ 74,144.43. Difference: **GH₵ 21,992.14**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** For Offer D, what is the opportunity cost over two years of GH₵ 120,000 that could have earned 9.45% a year?

<Solution>

120,000 × 1.0945² − 120,000 = **GH₵ 23,751.63** (about 11,340 in the first year, more in the second as interest compounds). If he pays in instalments, it is less, because only part of the money is tied up at first.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Esi has no emergency fund and owes GH₵ 3,000 on a loan at 3% a month. Is Offer A (the Treasury bill) a good use of her GH₵ 5,000 of savings?

<Solution>

Not first. The loan costs 1.03¹² − 1 = 42.6% a year, far more than the bill’s 9.45%; and she needs an emergency fund. Paying off the loan, then building an emergency fund, comes first. (Question 9: fit.)

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Write two questions you would ask the fund manager in Offer B before investing.

<Solution>

For example: **Why has the fund trailed its benchmark**, and what would change that? **Is there a cheaper fund** (or index fund) with similar holdings? Others: who is the custodian; what is the total cost including trading; how quickly are sales paid.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** A friend’s “cocoa export” scheme pays 8% a month, is registered as a company, and has paid her on time for a year. Use the checklist to explain why that is not reassuring.

<Solution>

**Company registration is not a licence** to take investments. **8% a month** is 151.8% a year (1.08¹² − 1), impossible from cocoa trading. **Paying on time** is exactly what a Ponzi scheme does until new money slows. The questions that matter (licence, source of return, comparison with a bill, worst case) all point to fraud.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Choose one investment you have been offered or seen advertised, and answer the ten questions. What is your verdict?

<Solution>

Answers will vary. A good answer gives a short, specific reply to each question, notes where information is missing, and reaches a verdict (sound, legitimate but expensive or ill-fitting, high-risk, or likely fraud) with reasons.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Ask ten questions of every offer: **what it is, who and licensed, where the return comes from, comparison, worst case, costs, getting out, tax, fit, how much**; and check your own state of mind.
- **Treasury bill**: sound, for short-term and safe money.
- **Licensed share fund**: legitimate; judge its costs and record against its benchmark.
- **Guaranteed high returns with recruitment bonuses**: almost certainly fraud.
- **Land in instalments**: possibly sound, but only after the title is proved; illiquid and concentrated.
- Write the answers down, get documents, take your time, and ask someone independent.
