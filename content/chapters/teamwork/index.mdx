## From idea to release

Work usually arrives as a **ticket** describing a problem. Before writing code, make sure you understand who it is for and how you will know it is done. Many teams work in short cycles, often called sprints, with a brief daily check-in and a board showing what is to do, in progress and done. The labels vary; the point is small steps and frequent feedback.

## Requirements

The most expensive bugs are building the wrong thing. Ask questions early. Repeat the request back in your own words. Agree on examples: “when the cart is empty, the button is disabled”.

## Code review

**As the author**: keep the change small, explain what and why in the description, review your own diff first, and receive comments as help with the code, not judgement of you.

**As the reviewer**: look for correctness, clarity, tests and security. Be specific and kind. Ask questions (“what happens if this list is empty?”). Say what is good too. Do not block on personal taste.

## Estimating

Estimates are guesses. Break the work into pieces of a day or less, give a range, and say immediately when the range turns out wrong. Bad news early is a professional skill.

## Writing

Engineers write constantly: a README that gets a newcomer running in ten minutes, a design document that states the problem and the options before the choice, a bug report with steps, expected and actual results. Clear writing is clear thinking made visible.

## Technical debt

Shortcuts taken to ship faster are a loan. Some debt is sensible. Unpaid, the interest is every later change taking longer. Record it and pay it down steadily.

## Being good to work with

Ask for help after a fair attempt, and show what you tried. Say “I do not know” without embarrassment. Share credit. Take responsibility when you break something. Help the next person learn what you just learned.
