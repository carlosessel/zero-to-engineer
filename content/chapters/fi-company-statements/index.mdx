If you own a share, you own part of a business, and the business reports how it is doing in its financial statements. You have already drawn up the same three statements for a household (<Ref to="fi-household-statements" />) and built them from a ledger (<Ref to="fi-double-entry" />). By the end of this chapter you will be able to find a listed company’s statements, read its income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement line by line, see how the three fit together, tell profit from cash, and spot the warning signs that deserve a closer look. Every figure here belongs to a fictional company; this is general education, not advice on any company.

## Where to find the statements

Listed companies must publish **audited** accounts every year, and usually shorter unaudited results every half-year or quarter. An **annual report** typically contains:

- a **chair’s and chief executive’s review**: the story management wants to tell;
- a **strategic report**: the business, its markets and its risks;
- the **auditor’s report**: an independent accountant’s opinion on whether the statements are fair;
- the **financial statements** themselves;
- the **notes**, which explain the figures, accounting choices, debts, and anything unusual.

Listed companies in Ghana and the UK prepare their accounts under **International Financial Reporting Standards** (IFRS), so the layout is broadly the same. Figures are usually shown for this year and last year side by side.

The company in this chapter is **Volta Foods PLC**, the fictional food producer from <Ref to="fi-markets" /> and <Ref to="fi-shares" />, with 120 million shares priced at GH₵ 4.05. All figures are in **GH₵ million**.

## The income statement

The **income statement** (or statement of profit or loss) shows a year’s income and expenses.

| | 2026 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 300.0 | 260.0 |
| Cost of sales | (195.0) | (172.0) |
| **Gross profit** | **105.0** | **88.0** |
| Operating expenses | (60.0) | (52.0) |
| **Operating profit** | **45.0** | **36.0** |
| Finance costs | (13.0) | (12.0) |
| **Profit before tax** | **32.0** | **24.0** |
| Income tax | (8.0) | (6.0) |
| **Profit for the year** | **24.0** | **18.0** |
| Earnings per share (GH₵) | 0.20 | 0.15 |

Line by line:

- **Revenue** (turnover, sales): what customers were charged for goods and services in the year, whether or not they have paid yet.
- **Cost of sales**: the direct cost of what was sold, such as ingredients, packaging and factory wages. Figures in brackets are subtracted.
- **Gross profit** = revenue − cost of sales.
- **Operating expenses**: running the business beyond making the product: distribution, marketing, offices, management, and **depreciation** (here GH₵ 15 million), the share of long-lived assets’ cost charged to this year.
- **Operating profit** = gross profit − operating expenses: what the business itself earned, before the cost of its debt and tax.
- **Finance costs**: mainly interest on borrowings.
- **Profit before tax**, then **income tax**, then **profit for the year** (net profit, or earnings), which belongs to the shareholders.
- **Earnings per share** (EPS) = profit ÷ shares = 24 ÷ 120 = GH₵ 0.20 (<Ref to="fi-shares" />).

Revenue grew 300 ÷ 260 − 1 = **15.4%**, but profit grew 24 ÷ 18 − 1 = **33.3%**, because costs grew more slowly than sales. The next chapter turns these into ratios.

## The balance sheet

The **balance sheet** (statement of financial position) is a snapshot at the year end of what the company owns and owes. As in every balance sheet, **assets = liabilities + equity**.

| At 31 December | 2026 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Property, plant and equipment | 150.0 | 140.0 |
| Inventories | 40.0 | 35.0 |
| Trade receivables | 35.0 | 30.0 |
| Cash | 4.6 | 18.0 |
| **Total assets** | **229.6** | **223.0** |
| Trade payables | 30.0 | 28.0 |
| Borrowings | 90.0 | 95.0 |
| **Total liabilities** | **120.0** | **123.0** |
| Share capital | 60.0 | 60.0 |
| Retained earnings | 49.6 | 40.0 |
| **Total equity** | **109.6** | **100.0** |
| **Total liabilities and equity** | **229.6** | **223.0** |

- **Non-current assets** are kept for more than a year: here, **property, plant and equipment** (factories, machines, lorries), shown at cost less depreciation so far.
- **Current assets** turn into cash within a year: **inventories** (stock of ingredients and finished food), **trade receivables** (money customers owe, <Ref to="fi-double-entry" />), and **cash**.
- **Current liabilities** are due within a year: here, **trade payables** (money owed to suppliers).
- **Non-current liabilities**: here, **borrowings**, bank loans and bonds due after more than a year.
- **Equity**: the shareholders’ part. **Share capital** is what shareholders paid in for their shares; **retained earnings** are the profits kept in the business over the years rather than paid out.

Retained earnings link the balance sheet to the income statement: last year’s 40.0, plus this year’s profit of 24.0, minus dividends paid of 14.4 (GH₵ 0.12 × 120 million shares), gives **49.6**.

## The cash flow statement

Profit is not cash, as <Ref to="fi-household-statements" /> showed. The **cash flow statement** shows where cash actually came from and went, in three parts.

| 2026 | |
|---|---|
| Profit for the year | 24.0 |
| Add back depreciation | 15.0 |
| Increase in inventories | (5.0) |
| Increase in trade receivables | (5.0) |
| Increase in trade payables | 2.0 |
| **Cash from operating activities** | **31.0** |
| Purchase of property, plant and equipment | (25.0) |
| **Cash from investing activities** | **(25.0)** |
| Repayment of borrowings | (5.0) |
| Dividends paid | (14.4) |
| **Cash from financing activities** | **(19.4)** |
| **Net change in cash** | **(13.4)** |
| Cash at 1 January | 18.0 |
| **Cash at 31 December** | **4.6** |

Reading the operating section:

- **Depreciation is added back** because it was subtracted to get profit but no cash left the company for it this year; the cash left when the machines were bought.
- **More inventory** (35 to 40) used cash: food made but not yet sold.
- **More receivables** (30 to 35) means some of the year’s sales have not yet been paid for, so profit counted cash that has not arrived.
- **More payables** (28 to 30) kept cash in: Volta has not yet paid some suppliers.

The company made GH₵ 24 million of profit and GH₵ 31 million of cash from operations, which is healthy. But it spent 25 on new equipment, repaid 5 of its loans and paid 14.4 in dividends, so its cash fell from 18.0 to just **4.6**.

**Free cash flow**, operating cash flow minus spending on equipment, was 31 − 25 = **GH₵ 6 million**. The dividend of 14.4 was more than twice that: it was paid partly out of the cash pile, which cannot happen for long.

The checks in Python (optional), which confirm the balance sheet balances and the cash flow explains the change in cash:

```python
assets = {2025: 140 + 35 + 30 + 18.0, 2026: 150 + 40 + 35 + 4.6}
liab_equity = {2025: 28 + 95 + 60 + 40.0, 2026: 30 + 90 + 60 + 49.6}
operating = 24.0 + 15 - (40 - 35) - (35 - 30) + (30 - 28)
investing = -25
financing = -5 - 0.12 * 120
for year in (2025, 2026):
    print(year, round(assets[year], 1), round(liab_equity[year], 1))
print(round(operating, 1), investing, round(financing, 1), round(18 + operating + investing + financing, 1))
```

```text
2025 223.0 223.0
2026 229.6 229.6
31.0 -25 -19.4 4.6
```

## How the three statements fit together

- **Profit** from the income statement flows into **retained earnings** on the balance sheet, after dividends.
- **Depreciation** reduces profit and the value of equipment on the balance sheet, and is added back in the cash flow statement.
- Changes in **inventories, receivables and payables** on the balance sheet explain the gap between profit and operating cash.
- **Equipment bought** appears in investing cash flow and increases property, plant and equipment.
- **Borrowing and repayments** appear in financing cash flow and change borrowings on the balance sheet.
- The **net change in cash** matches the change in cash on the balance sheet.

If any of these do not tie up in a real company’s accounts, the notes should explain why.

## Warning signs

None of these proves a problem, but each is worth reading the notes for:

- **The auditor’s opinion is not clean**: a “qualified” opinion, an “emphasis of matter” about **going concern** (doubt that the company can keep operating), or a change of auditor without a clear reason.
- **Profit keeps rising but operating cash does not**, year after year.
- **Receivables growing much faster than revenue**: customers are not paying, or sales are being recorded too early.
- **Inventories piling up** faster than sales.
- **Dividends paid from borrowing or cash reserves** rather than from free cash flow, as Volta did this year.
- **Large “one-off” or “exceptional” items every year**, which are then not really one-off.
- **Transactions with related parties**, such as directors’ other companies.
- **Debt rising fast**, or loans due soon with little cash to repay them.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading only the headline profit**, not cash flow or the balance sheet.
- **Skipping the notes**, where the important details usually are.
- **Treating revenue as cash received**, or profit as money in the bank.
- **Ignoring the auditor’s report.**
- **Looking at one year only**, instead of the trend over several.
- **Taking management’s review at face value**: it is written to persuade.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** In 2025, what was Volta’s gross profit as a share of revenue?

<Solution>

88 ÷ 260 = **33.8%**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Show that Volta’s 2025 balance sheet balances.

<Solution>

Assets: 140 + 35 + 30 + 18 = **223**. Liabilities and equity: 28 + 95 + 60 + 40 = **223**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** If Volta had paid no dividend in 2026, what would its cash and retained earnings have been at the year end?

<Solution>

Cash: 4.6 + 14.4 = **19.0**. Retained earnings: 49.6 + 14.4 = **64.0**. Both sides of the balance sheet rise by 14.4.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Why does an increase in trade receivables reduce operating cash flow?

<Solution>

Revenue, and so profit, includes sales customers have **not yet paid for**. The increase in receivables is profit that has not arrived as cash, so it is subtracted to get from profit to cash.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** A company reports profit of GH₵ 50 million, depreciation of 10, an increase in receivables of 30 and an increase in inventories of 20. What is its operating cash flow (ignoring other items)? What would you want to know?

<Solution>

50 + 10 − 30 − 20 = **GH₵ 10 million**. Profit is five times cash. Why are receivables and inventories growing so fast? Are customers paying? Is stock selling? Read the notes and compare with revenue growth.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Volta’s depreciation was 15 and it bought 25 of new equipment. What happened to property, plant and equipment on the balance sheet?

<Solution>

140 + 25 − 15 = **150**, matching the balance sheet.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** If Volta keeps paying GH₵ 14.4 million a year in dividends with free cash flow of GH₵ 6 million, what happens? What are its options?

<Solution>

It will run out of cash within a year: the shortfall is 8.4 a year against 4.6 of cash. It must **cut the dividend**, **borrow** more, **sell shares**, **spend less on equipment**, or raise its operating cash flow. Paying dividends from borrowing is a warning sign.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** An auditor’s report includes an “emphasis of matter” paragraph about going concern. What does it mean, and what would you read next?

<Solution>

The auditor is drawing attention to **significant doubt that the company can keep operating**, usually because of debts it may not be able to pay or refinance. Read the note it refers to: which debts fall due, what cash and facilities the company has, and management’s plans.

</Solution>

## Summary

- An **annual report** has management’s review, the **auditor’s report**, the statements and the **notes**; listed companies in Ghana and the UK use **IFRS**.
- **Income statement**: revenue → gross profit → operating profit → profit before tax → profit; EPS.
- **Balance sheet**: assets = liabilities + equity at the year end; **retained earnings** link it to profit and dividends.
- **Cash flow statement**: operating, investing and financing cash; profit adjusted for depreciation and working capital gives operating cash.
- **Free cash flow** = operating cash flow − spending on equipment; dividends above it cannot last.
- Read the trend, the notes and the auditor’s report, and watch for profit that never turns into cash.
