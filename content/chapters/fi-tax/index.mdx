After costs, tax is the next thing between an investment’s return and what you keep. Tax rules differ from country to country and change in almost every budget, so this chapter teaches the ideas that stay the same: how investment income is taxed, how to compare a taxed return with a tax-free one, why tax on gains is paid only when you sell, how inflation and tax combine, and why tax-advantaged accounts are worth using first. The country notes give the current position in Ghana and the UK; always check the tax authority’s own guidance before acting. This is general education, not tax advice.

## What gets taxed

Investments produce four kinds of taxable income:

- **Interest**, from savings, bills and bonds.
- **Dividends**, from shares and some funds.
- **Rent**, from property.
- **Capital gains**: the profit when you sell something for more than you paid.

Tax on them is collected in one of two ways. **Withholding tax** is taken by the payer before you receive the money, as with dividends in Ghana. A **final** withholding tax settles the matter; a non-final one is a payment towards a bill you work out later. Other income is declared on a **tax return** and the tax is paid afterwards.

Many systems also give **allowances** (amounts you can receive tax-free each year) or **exemptions** (kinds of income that are not taxed at all). Where investment income is added to your other income, it is taxed at your **marginal rate**, the rate on your next cedi of income (<Ref to="fi-income" />).

## Comparing taxed and tax-free returns

What matters is the return **after tax**: the rate × (1 − tax rate).

**Worked example.** Esi pays tax at 40% on any extra interest. A savings account pays 5%, taxable; a tax-free account pays 4.2%.

- Taxed account after tax: 5% × (1 − 0.40) = **3.0%**.
- Tax-free account: **4.2%**.

The lower headline rate is the better deal. To compare in the other direction, the **tax-equivalent yield** is the taxed rate that would match a tax-free one: 4.2% ÷ (1 − 0.40) = **7%**. The taxed account would need to pay 7% to match.

**Worked example (Ghana).** Kojo compares a 364-day Treasury bill paying 9.447%, whose interest is exempt for individuals, with a bank share whose dividend yield is 10%. Dividends suffer the 8% withholding tax, so the dividend gives him 10% × 0.92 = **9.2%**, before any change in the share price. The headline gap of more than half a point has become a gap the other way, and the share carries far more risk (<Ref to="fi-risk-return" />).

## Capital gains

A **capital gain** is the sale price minus what you paid, minus allowable costs such as commission. A fall gives a **capital loss**, which in many systems can be set against gains.

Two features matter:

1. **Gains are usually taxed only when you sell** (when they are **realised**). A share that has doubled but not been sold has an unrealised gain and no tax yet.
2. Because tax is put off until you sell, the money that would have gone in tax keeps growing for you in the meantime. This is the value of **deferral**.

**Worked example.** GH₵ 10,000 grows at 8% a year for 20 years, in a fictional system that taxes returns at 20%.

- **Taxed every year** (as interest often is), it grows at 8% × 0.8 = 6.4% a year: 10,000 × 1.064²⁰ = **GH₵ 34,580.60**.
- **Taxed only at the end** (as a gain often is): it grows to 10,000 × 1.08²⁰ = 46,609.57, and tax of 20% on the gain of 36,609.57 leaves **GH₵ 39,287.66**.

The same rate of tax, paid later, leaves GH₵ 4,707.06 more. Holding long-term investments rather than trading them often is good for costs *and* for tax.

## Inflation makes tax heavier

Tax is charged on **nominal** income, but what matters is **real** return (<Ref to="fi-inflation" />). When inflation is high, tax can turn a positive real return negative.

**Worked example.** A bond pays 25% interest, inflation is 20%, and the interest is taxed at 30%. After tax the bond pays 25% × 0.7 = 17.5%. Real return after tax: 1.175 ÷ 1.20 − 1 = **−2.08%**. Before tax it was a positive 4.17%. The tax took more than the whole real return, because part of the “interest” was only making up for inflation. This is one reason some countries exempt interest on government securities.

## Tax-advantaged accounts

Governments encourage saving by giving some accounts special treatment:

- **Tax-free savings and investment accounts**, such as the UK’s Individual Savings Account (ISA): no tax on interest, dividends or gains inside the account, up to a yearly limit on what you pay in.
- **Pensions**: in many countries, contributions are made before tax (or the government adds tax relief), and the fund grows without tax. Some or all of the withdrawals are then taxed in retirement. Paying in at a 40% rate and drawing out at 20% is a large gain (<Ref to="fi-retirement" />).
- **Employer schemes** where the employer also contributes.

A sensible order, for most people, is: take any employer contribution in full; then use tax-advantaged accounts; and only then invest in ordinary taxable accounts. Check the rules on getting money out, which can be strict, especially for pensions.

## Your obligations

- **Avoidance and evasion are different.** Using allowances and accounts the law provides is legal and expected. **Tax evasion**, hiding income or lying to the tax authority, is a crime. Aggressive schemes promising to make tax disappear often end up as the second.
- **Keep records** of what you paid for each investment, what you sold it for, and income received, for as long as your tax authority requires (<Ref to="fi-bookkeeping" />).
- **Foreign income** may be taxable at home as well as where it arises; double tax treaties often prevent paying twice on the same income.
- **When in doubt, ask** the tax authority or a qualified adviser. Rules change, often.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing headline rates** of taxed and tax-free products.
- **Forgetting withholding tax** on dividends or foreign income.
- **Selling winners every year** and paying tax and costs that holding would have deferred.
- **Ignoring tax-advantaged accounts**, or filling ordinary accounts first.
- **Not keeping records** of purchase prices, and being unable to work out a gain years later.
- **Falling for schemes that promise to make tax disappear.**

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Interest of 8% is taxed at 20%. What is the after-tax return?

<Solution>

8% × 0.8 = **6.4%**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** You buy shares for GH₵ 6,000, paying GH₵ 60 commission, and sell them for GH₵ 9,000, paying GH₵ 90. What is the capital gain?

<Solution>

9,000 − 90 − (6,000 + 60) = **GH₵ 2,850**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** What taxed rate matches a tax-free 6% for someone paying 25% on extra income?

<Solution>

6% ÷ (1 − 0.25) = **8%**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A share has a 7% dividend yield and the dividend is subject to 8% withholding tax. What does the investor receive as a yield after tax?

<Solution>

7% × 0.92 = **6.44%**.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Ama holds shares worth GH₵ 15,000 that cost GH₵ 9,000. She has not sold. Does she owe tax on the gain this year, in a system that taxes gains on sale?

<Solution>

**No.** The GH₵ 6,000 gain is **unrealised**; tax is due only when she sells (and then on the gain at that time).

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Interest is 18%, inflation 15% and tax on interest 25%. What is the real return after tax?

<Solution>

After tax: 18% × 0.75 = 13.5%. Real: 1.135 ÷ 1.15 − 1 = **−1.30%**. Before tax the real return was 1.18 ÷ 1.15 − 1 = 2.61%.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Kwame can save in an ordinary account at 6% taxed at 40%, or in a pension where he gets 40% tax relief on what he pays in and pays 20% tax when he draws out. Explain why the pension is likely better if both grow at the same rate before tax.

<Solution>

In the pension, money goes in **before tax** (relief at 40%), grows **without tax** each year, and is taxed at only **20%** on the way out. In the ordinary account, he pays in money already taxed at 40% and then loses 40% of each year’s interest, so it grows at only 3.6% a year. The pension gives him more to invest, faster growth, and a lower tax rate at the end, though the money is locked away until pension age.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Using the deferral example, what would GH₵ 10,000 at 8% for 20 years leave if taxed at 20% every year, compared with 20% on the gain at the end? Why are they different, when the tax rate is the same?

<Solution>

Taxed yearly: **GH₵ 34,580.60**. Taxed at the end: **GH₵ 39,287.66**. With yearly tax, the money paid in tax each year can never grow again. With tax at the end, that money stays invested for the whole 20 years, earning returns on itself, and is only paid over at the end.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Investments produce **interest, dividends, rent and capital gains**; tax is collected by **withholding** or on a **tax return**, often with **allowances** and **exemptions**.
- Compare returns **after tax**: rate × (1 − tax rate). The **tax-equivalent yield** of a tax-free rate is rate ÷ (1 − tax rate).
- **Capital gains** are usually taxed only when **realised**; putting tax off (**deferral**) lets more money grow.
- Tax on nominal interest during high inflation can make the real return negative.
- Use **tax-advantaged accounts** and employer contributions first.
- Avoidance using the law’s allowances is legal; **evasion** is a crime. Keep records.
