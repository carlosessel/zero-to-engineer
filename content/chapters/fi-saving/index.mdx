Savings are what stand between an unexpected bill and an expensive loan, and between a goal and a wish. By the end of this chapter you will be able to turn a goal into a monthly amount, decide how big an emergency fund should be and build it in stages, and compare the places savings can be kept by safety, access and return.

## Why save at all

Saving does three jobs:

1. **Emergencies.** A medical bill, a broken phone, a lost job or a family crisis will happen to almost everyone. With savings it is a problem; without them it becomes a debt, often at a high interest rate, which makes the next month harder still.
2. **Goals.** A laptop, a deposit on a flat, school fees, a wedding, starting a business. Saving first and buying later costs less than borrowing now and paying interest.
3. **A bridge to investing.** Money for long-term investing should come from savings you will not need soon, as <Ref to="fi-the-map" /> explained.

## Turning a goal into a monthly amount

A goal you can act on has an amount and a date. From those, the monthly saving needed is:

monthly amount = goal ÷ number of months

**Worked example.** Esi wants a GH₵ 6,000 laptop in 15 months. She needs 6,000 ÷ 15 = **GH₵ 400 a month**. If that does not fit her budget, she has three levers: a smaller amount (a cheaper laptop), a later date (more months), or a bigger monthly amount (cut something else). Writing the sum down makes the trade-off visible.

This ignores interest, which keeps the arithmetic simple and errs on the safe side: any interest earned gets her there a little sooner.

## Pay yourself first, automatically

<Ref to="fi-budgeting" /> introduced **paying yourself first**: moving savings out on pay day, before spending begins. The most reliable way to do it is to make it automatic. A **standing order** is an instruction to your bank to move a fixed amount to another account on a fixed date, every month, until you stop it. Many banks and mobile money services can do the same. Once it is set up, saving happens without needing willpower each month.

## The emergency fund

An **emergency fund** is money kept only for real emergencies: unexpected, necessary and urgent. A medical bill is an emergency; a sale on trainers is not. Its size is measured in months of **essential spending**, the needs in your budget, not your whole income.

A common rule of thumb is **three to six months of essential spending**. Aim towards the higher end if:

- your income is irregular, or your job is insecure;
- other people depend on your income;
- you are self-employed, with no sick pay.

**Worked example.** Ama’s essential spending, from her zero-based budget, is GH₵ 3,260 a month. Three months is 3 × 3,260 = **GH₵ 9,780**; six months is 6 × 3,260 = **GH₵ 19,560**. At GH₵ 400 a month, three months would take 9,780 ÷ 400 = 24.45, so about **25 months** to save.

That is a long time, and that is normal. Build it in stages:

1. **A starter fund** of one month’s essentials (GH₵ 3,260 for Ama). This alone covers most everyday emergencies.
2. Then pay off any expensive debt (<Ref to="fi-debt" /> explains why).
3. Then build to three months, and later to six if your situation calls for it.

When you use the fund, that is what it is for. Then rebuild it before going back to other goals.

## Where to keep savings

Three properties matter for savings, and you usually have to trade them against each other:

- Safety: will the money still be there, in full?
- **Liquidity**: how quickly can you turn it into cash you can spend, without losing any of it? Cash in your hand is perfectly liquid; land can take months to sell.
- **Return**: how much does it earn? For savings, this is mostly **interest**, the payment a bank or borrower makes you for using your money. An interest rate of 10% a year on GH₵ 5,000 pays 0.10 × 5,000 = GH₵ 500 over a year. <Ref to="fi-numbers" /> showed how rates add up over time; <Ref to="fi-interest" /> goes further.

| Where | Safety | Liquidity | Return | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash at home | Low (theft, fire, temptation) | Instant | None | A small amount for daily needs |
| Current account or mobile money wallet | High at a licensed provider | Instant | None or very little | Spending money; pay day |
| Savings account | High at a licensed bank | Quick | Some interest | Emergency fund, near goals |
| Fixed deposit | High at a licensed bank | Locked until a set date; early withdrawal may lose interest | More interest than a savings account | Goals with a known date |
| Government treasury bills | High in most countries | Locked for weeks or months; can sometimes be sold | Interest set at purchase | Goals with a known date |

A **fixed deposit** (in some countries a fixed-term or time deposit) pays a higher rate in exchange for leaving the money untouched for a set period. A **treasury bill** is a short loan to the government; the bonds chapter covers them.

The emergency fund belongs at the safe, liquid end: a savings account at a licensed bank is the usual choice, because you need it quickly and in full. Money for a goal with a fixed date can go somewhere less liquid that pays more, as long as it unlocks before you need it. Money you will not need for many years can be invested, accepting more risk.

What about safety? Banks and mobile money providers are licensed and supervised, and many countries also **protect deposits** up to a limit if a bank fails. <Ref to="fi-banking" /> covers how this works and what the limits are.

### Savings groups and collectors

In many communities people save together. In a **rotating savings group**, each member pays the same amount into a pot each month, and each month one member takes the whole pot, until everyone has had a turn. With 10 members paying GH₵ 200, each member pays 10 × 200 = GH₵ 2,000 in total over the ten months and receives GH₵ 2,000 once. Those early in the order effectively get an interest-free loan; those late in the order have saved, with no interest. The group works on trust: if a member stops paying after taking the pot, the others lose.

In Ghana, **susu** collectors visit traders and workers to collect small daily savings, returning them at the end of a period minus a fee, commonly one day’s contribution for each month. Susu is a long-established and widely used way to save, but informal collectors are not supervised, and people have lost savings when collectors disappeared. If you use one, use a licensed company, keep records of every payment, and do not keep more with them than you could afford to lose.

<Note>

**Country notes (checked November 2026).** **Ghana:** before using any savings company, susu company or microfinance institution, check that it appears on the Bank of Ghana’s list of licensed institutions. Deposit protection is covered in the banking chapter. **United Kingdom:** interest from savings is taxed above certain allowances, but interest in an Individual Savings Account (ISA) is tax-free. In the 2026 to 2027 tax year you can put up to £20,000 into ISAs in total. Check GOV.UK for the current rules, which change in budgets.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **No emergency fund, so every surprise becomes debt.** Even a small starter fund breaks this cycle.
- **Keeping the emergency fund somewhere hard to reach** (a fixed deposit, an investment, land), so it is not there when the emergency is.
- **Spending the emergency fund on non-emergencies.** Keep it in a separate account, ideally not linked to your spending card.
- **Saving without a target.** “Save more” is easy to postpone; “GH₵ 400 a month by standing order for 15 months” is not.
- **Chasing the highest rate at an unlicensed institution.** A rate far above what licensed banks pay is a warning sign, not a bargain.
- **Never rebuilding after using the fund.** Top it up before returning to other goals.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Esi wants GH₵ 6,000 in 15 months. How much must she save each month?

<Solution>

6,000 ÷ 15 = **GH₵ 400** a month.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Kojo’s essential spending is GH₵ 2,800 a month. What are three and six months of essentials?

<Solution>

3 × 2,800 = **GH₵ 8,400**; 6 × 2,800 = **GH₵ 16,800**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Kojo saves GH₵ 350 a month towards a starter fund of one month’s essentials. How many months will it take?

<Solution>

2,800 ÷ 350 = **8 months**.

</Solution>

**4. (warm-up)** How much interest does GH₵ 5,000 earn in a year at 10% a year, if the interest is paid once at the end of the year?

<Solution>

0.10 × 5,000 = **GH₵ 500**.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** A savings account pays 8% a year and a one-year fixed deposit pays 18% a year, both paid once at the end of the year. On GH₵ 10,000, how much more does the fixed deposit pay, and what do you give up for it?

<Solution>

Fixed deposit: 0.18 × 10,000 = 1,800. Savings account: 0.08 × 10,000 = 800. The difference is **GH₵ 1,000**. In exchange you give up **liquidity**: the money is locked for the year, and taking it out early may cost some or all of the interest.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Abena saves 15% of her GH₵ 3,500 net pay. How long until she has GH₵ 6,300?

<Solution>

0.15 × 3,500 = GH₵ 525 a month. 6,300 ÷ 525 = **12 months**.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** Rank these from most to least liquid: a one-year fixed deposit, a plot of land, cash in a wallet, a savings account.

<Solution>

**Cash in a wallet** (instant), **savings account** (quick), **one-year fixed deposit** (locked until it matures), **land** (can take months to sell, and a quick sale may mean a lower price).

</Solution>

**8. (standard)** Which of these are emergencies for an emergency fund? (a) An urgent medical bill. (b) A friend’s wedding announced eight months ahead. (c) A broken phone that you need for work. (d) A half-price sale on a television.

<Solution>

(a) and (c) are emergencies: unexpected, necessary and urgent. (b) is a **planned** cost: give it a sinking fund. (d) is a want, however good the price.

</Solution>

**9. (standard)** In a rotating savings group of 10 members, each pays GH₵ 200 a month. How much does each member receive, and when is it best and worst to be in the order?

<Solution>

Each receives 10 × 200 = **GH₵ 2,000** once. Early in the order is like an interest-free loan (you get the money before you have paid it all in); late is like saving with no interest, and carries the most risk, because others have already received their pot and might stop paying.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** Akosua, the tailor with an irregular income, decides her emergency fund should be six months of essential spending at the level of her lowest month, GH₵ 1,900. How much is that, and why might she choose six months rather than three?

<Solution>

6 × 1,900 = **GH₵ 11,400**. She is self-employed with an irregular income and no sick pay, so a run of bad months or an illness hits her harder than an employee; the longer cushion matches the bigger risk.

</Solution>

**11. (stretch)** Ama wants a three-month fund of GH₵ 9,780. How many months does it take at GH₵ 400 a month, and at GH₵ 600 a month?

<Solution>

9,780 ÷ 400 = 24.45, so **25 months** (after 24 she is still GH₵ 180 short). 9,780 ÷ 600 = 16.3, so **17 months**.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Savings turn emergencies into problems rather than debts, and make goals affordable without interest.
- A goal needs an amount and a date: monthly amount = goal ÷ months.
- Pay yourself first, automatically, with a **standing order**.
- An **emergency fund** covers three to six months of **essential spending**; build it in stages, starting with one month.
- Where you keep savings trades safety, **liquidity** and **return**; emergency money belongs somewhere safe and liquid.
- A **fixed deposit** pays more for locking money away; **interest** is the payment for lending your money.
- Savings groups and **susu** collectors help people save but depend on trust; use licensed providers and keep records.
