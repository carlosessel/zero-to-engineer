You cannot control what markets do, but you can control what you pay. Costs are the one part of investing where less is reliably better: every cedi paid in fees is a cedi that no longer earns anything for you, for the rest of your life. By the end of this chapter you will be able to name every kind of cost an investor pays, add them into one yearly total, choose between percentage and flat fees, see how much of your *real* return fees take, and ask the questions that bring hidden costs into the open. This is general education, not advice on any provider.

## The kinds of cost

Investment costs come in two kinds: those charged **every year** on your whole balance, and those charged **each time you act**.

Every year:

- **Fund charges**: the ongoing charge (or expense ratio) of each fund you hold, taken from inside the fund (<Ref to="fi-funds" />).
- **Platform or account fees**: what a broker, bank or investment app charges to hold your investments, as a percentage of your balance or a flat amount.
- **Advice fees**: if you pay an adviser a yearly percentage to look after your money.
- **Costs inside a fund**: what the fund pays to trade, which are often not in its headline charge.

Each time you act:

- **Commission**: the broker’s charge per trade (<Ref to="fi-markets" />).
- **The bid-ask spread**: you buy at the ask and sell at the bid.
- **Entry and exit fees**: a percentage taken when you put money into, or take it out of, some funds and policies.
- **Currency conversion**: a margin on the exchange rate when you buy something priced in another currency, often hidden in the rate (<Ref to="fi-banking" />).
- **Taxes and levies** on transactions, in some countries.

And sometimes:

- **Performance fees**: a share of the gains, usually above a target, charged by some funds.
- **Penalties** for early withdrawal from fixed deposits, pensions or insurance-linked savings.

## Add them up

**Worked example.** Ama has GH₵ 20,000 in one fund through an investment app. The fund charges 1.5% a year and the app 0.5%. Her yearly costs: 20,000 × 1.5% = 300, plus 20,000 × 0.5% = 100, so **GH₵ 400 a year, 2% of her money**, before any trading costs. She never sees a bill for either; both are taken from her balance.

Always add the fund charge and the platform fee together. A “cheap” fund on an expensive platform, or a free platform selling expensive funds, can cost as much as anything else.

## Percentage or flat fees

A **percentage fee** grows with your balance. A **flat fee** stays the same however much you have. Which is cheaper depends on the size of your pot.

**Worked example.** Platform A charges 0.45% a year; Platform B charges a flat GH₵ 120 a year. They cost the same when 0.45% of the balance equals 120: 120 ÷ 0.0045 = **GH₵ 26,666.67**. Below that, A is cheaper; above it, B is. With GH₵ 100,000, A costs 450 a year and B still 120.

Flat **per-trade** fees work the other way for small amounts: a GH₵ 20 commission is 2% of a GH₵ 1,000 purchase but 0.2% of GH₵ 10,000. If trades carry a fixed charge, invest less often, in larger amounts.

## Entry fees need a gain just to break even

**Worked example.** A fund takes a 5% entry fee. Kojo invests GH₵ 10,000, and 9,500 is invested. To get back to 10,000 the fund must rise 10,000 ÷ 9,500 − 1 = **5.26%**. Before he has earned anything, he is a year or more behind. Ask for funds with no entry fee, or for the fee to be waived.

## Small fees, decades, and real returns

As <Ref to="fi-funds" /> showed, fees compound. Over 30 years at 10% a year, GH₵ 10,000 grows to GH₵ 174,494.02 with no costs. With total costs of 1.5% a year it reaches only **GH₵ 110,883.64**. The costs took GH₵ 63,610.38, almost **39% of the gain**, though 1.5% sounds small.

The sharper way to see fees is against your **real return** (<Ref to="fi-inflation" />). Suppose investments return 10% a year and inflation is 6%:

- Real return with no fees: 1.10 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = **3.77%** a year.
- With 2% a year in fees: 1.10 × 0.98 ÷ 1.06 − 1 = **1.70%** a year.

A 2% fee looks like a fifth of a 10% return. In fact it takes **55%** of the real return, the part that actually makes you richer. With 0.2% in fees the real return is 3.57%, and almost all of it is yours.

## Hidden and bundled costs

Some costs are designed to be hard to see:

- **Products that combine insurance and investment**, such as some savings policies and endowments, often have high charges spread across the policy, and heavy penalties for stopping early.
- **“Free” trading apps** may earn from wide spreads, poor exchange rates, or selling you riskier products.
- **Currency margins** hidden in an exchange rate rather than shown as a fee.
- **Trading costs inside a fund**, which come out of its returns but not its headline charge.

Ask any provider, in writing: *What will I pay in total, in cedis, each year, for GH₵ 10,000 invested? What does it cost to get out?* A provider that cannot or will not answer is telling you something.

## Cutting costs

- Prefer **low-cost index funds** where they exist, for the reasons in <Ref to="fi-funds" />.
- **Compare total costs**: fund charges plus platform fees plus trading costs.
- **Trade less.** Every trade has a cost, and most frequent trading does worse than holding.
- **Avoid entry and exit fees**, or ask for them to be waived.
- **Check costs every year.** Charges change, and a pot that has grown may be cheaper on a flat-fee platform.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking only at the fund charge** and ignoring platform, advice and trading costs.
- **Thinking 1% or 2% is too small to matter**, when over decades it takes a large share of the gain.
- **Paying for active management** that does not beat a cheaper index fund.
- **Trading often**, paying commission and spread each time.
- **Buying bundled savings and insurance products** without asking what they cost to stop.
- **Ignoring currency costs** on foreign investments.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** You hold GH₵ 50,000 in a fund charging 0.8% a year on a platform charging 0.3%. What are your yearly costs?

<Solution>

50,000 × (0.8% + 0.3%) = 50,000 × 1.1% = **GH₵ 550**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** A broker charges a flat GH₵ 25 per trade. What percentage is that of a GH₵ 500 purchase? Of GH₵ 5,000?

<Solution>

25 ÷ 500 = **5%**; 25 ÷ 5,000 = **0.5%**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Platform A charges 0.35% a year and Platform B a flat GH₵ 210 a year. At what balance do they cost the same? Which suits a pot of GH₵ 40,000?

<Solution>

210 ÷ 0.0035 = **GH₵ 60,000**. With 40,000, A costs 140 a year against B’s 210, so **A** is cheaper.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A fund has a 3% entry fee. What rise is needed to get back to the amount you paid in?

<Solution>

1 ÷ 0.97 − 1 = **3.09%**.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Investments return 12% a year and inflation is 8%. What is the real return with no fees, and with 2.5% of fees a year? What share of the real return do the fees take?

<Solution>

No fees: 1.12 ÷ 1.08 − 1 = **3.70%**. With fees: 1.12 × 0.975 ÷ 1.08 − 1 = **1.11%**. The fees take (3.70 − 1.11) ÷ 3.70 = **70%** of the real return.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** A friend says their investment app is free because it charges no commission. Name two ways it might still be making money from them.

<Solution>

Any two of: **wide spreads** on trades; a **margin on currency conversion**; **interest on cash** held in accounts; **payment from market makers** for its customers’ orders; selling **subscriptions** or riskier products such as leveraged trading.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Esi has GH₵ 30,000 and adds GH₵ 6,000 a year. She is choosing between a platform charging 0.4% a year and one charging a flat GH₵ 150. When does the flat fee start to win, roughly?

<Solution>

They cost the same at 150 ÷ 0.004 = GH₵ 37,500. She has 30,000 and adds 6,000 a year, so she passes 37,500 during her **second year** (sooner with growth). From then on, the flat fee is cheaper, and the gap widens as the pot grows.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** An adviser charges 1% a year on top of fund charges of 1.2%. The adviser says their advice “pays for itself”. What would you need to see to believe that?

<Solution>

Evidence that the advice adds at least **2.2% a year** of value after costs compared with a simple low-cost alternative: for example, by preventing costly mistakes (panic selling, unsuitable products), using tax allowances well, or planning that you could not do yourself. Ask what the total cost is in cedis each year, and what you would get for it.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Costs come **every year** (fund charges, platform, advice) and **each time you act** (commission, spread, entry and exit fees, currency margins).
- **Add them all up** to one yearly figure, in cedis and as a percentage.
- **Percentage fees** suit small pots; **flat fees** suit large ones. Per-trade flat fees favour fewer, larger trades.
- **Entry fees** need a gain just to get back to where you started.
- Fees compound: 1.5% a year took 39% of the gain over 30 years. Against the **real return**, a 2% fee took 55%.
- Ask for the **total cost in cedis** and the cost of leaving, in writing.
