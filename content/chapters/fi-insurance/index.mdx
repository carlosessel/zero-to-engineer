A plan for money can be wrecked in a day: a serious illness, a car crash, a fire, or the death of the person whose income a family depends on. Insurance exists so that a disaster like that does not become a financial disaster too. But insurance also costs more, on average, than it pays out, so buying too much of it, or the wrong kinds, wastes money. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain how insurance works and why it costs more than it pays on average, read the key terms in a policy, decide what is worth insuring and what is not, choose an excess, avoid being underinsured, and estimate how much life cover a family might need. This is general education, not advice on any policy.

## How insurance works

**Insurance** is a contract: you pay a regular amount, the **premium**, and the **insurer** agrees to pay you (or someone you name) if a specified event happens. It works by **pooling**: many people each pay a small, certain amount, and the few who suffer the loss are paid from the pool.

The insurer must also pay its staff, its sales agents and its shareholders. So across all its customers, premiums are larger than payouts. **On average, every insurance policy loses money for the person who buys it.** That is not a reason to avoid insurance; it is the price of turning a small chance of a ruinous loss into a certain, affordable cost.

**Worked example: phone insurance.** A phone worth GH₵ 2,000 can be insured for GH₵ 30 a month, GH₵ 360 a year, with an **excess** of GH₵ 300 (the part of each claim you pay yourself). Suppose 1 in 10 such phones is lost, stolen or broken each year. The average payout per customer is 0.10 × (2,000 − 300) = **GH₵ 170** a year. The customer pays 360 to receive 170 on average: they lose **GH₵ 190 a year**, on average. Losing a phone is annoying but not ruinous, so for someone with an emergency fund this is poor value.

## The rule: insure what would ruin you

Because insurance loses money on average, the question is never “could this happen?” but **“could I afford it if it did?”**

- **Insure** losses you could not cover from savings: serious illness, a house burning down, a car crash injuring others, the loss of your income through death or disability.
- **Don’t insure** losses you could cover from your emergency fund (<Ref to="fi-saving" />): a broken phone, a small repair, a cancelled trip. Paying these yourself is **self-insurance**, and over a lifetime it is much cheaper.

This is why an emergency fund and insurance work together: the fund handles small shocks, and insurance the large ones.

## Reading a policy

- **Premium**: what you pay, monthly or yearly.
- **Sum insured** (or cover): the most the insurer will pay.
- **Excess** (or deductible): the part of each claim you pay yourself.
- **Exclusions**: what is not covered, such as floods, pre-existing illnesses, or using your car for business. Read these first.
- **Insurance claim** (or claim): your request for payment after an event.
- **Policyholder** and **beneficiary**: the person who owns the policy, and the person who receives the payout, which differ in life insurance.
- **Disclosure**: you must answer the insurer’s questions truthfully. If you hide something relevant, such as a health condition, the insurer may refuse to pay a claim years later.

## Choosing an excess

A higher excess means you pay more of each claim, so the insurer charges a lower premium.

**Worked example.** Car insurance costs GH₵ 2,400 a year with a GH₵ 500 excess, or GH₵ 2,000 with a GH₵ 1,500 excess. Choosing the higher excess saves GH₵ 400 a year but costs GH₵ 1,000 more on any claim. If you claim about once every five years, over five years you save 400 × 5 = **GH₵ 2,000** in premiums and pay **GH₵ 1,000** more in excess. If you have an emergency fund to cover the excess, the higher one is cheaper.

## Underinsurance

If you insure something for less than it is worth, many policies apply an **average clause**: they pay only the same fraction of any claim as the fraction of the value you insured.

**Worked example.** A house would cost GH₵ 400,000 to rebuild but is insured for GH₵ 300,000, three-quarters of its value. A fire causes GH₵ 100,000 of damage. The insurer pays 100,000 × 300,000 ÷ 400,000 = **GH₵ 75,000**, not 100,000, even though the damage is well below the sum insured. Insure for the full cost of **rebuilding or replacing**, and update it as prices rise (<Ref to="fi-inflation" />).

## The main kinds

- **Health insurance**: pays for medical care. In Ghana, the National Health Insurance Scheme covers many treatments; private insurance can add more.
- **Life insurance**: pays a sum when the insured person dies. **Term insurance** pays only if death happens within a set term, say 20 years, and is the cheapest way to protect dependants. **Whole-of-life** and savings-linked policies pay out eventually but cost far more, mixing insurance with investment, often with high charges (<Ref to="fi-fees" />).
- **Income protection** or disability insurance: replaces part of your income if illness or injury stops you working.
- **Motor insurance**: third-party cover, for injury and damage you cause others, is a legal requirement in Ghana and the UK. Comprehensive cover also pays for your own car.
- **Home and contents insurance**: the building, and what is inside it.
- **Funeral and microinsurance**: small policies, often sold by mobile phone, covering funerals, hospital stays or crops.
- **Travel insurance**, mainly for medical costs abroad, which can be enormous.

Usually **not worth it**, for someone with an emergency fund: extended warranties on electronics, phone and gadget insurance, and insurance sold at the till alongside something else. They exist because they are profitable for the seller.

## How much life insurance?

Life insurance is for **people who depend on your income**. Someone with no dependants usually does not need it. One way to estimate how much is needed: the present value (<Ref to="fi-time-value" />) of the income your dependants would lose, minus savings they could use.

**Worked example.** Kofi’s family would need GH₵ 30,000 a year in today’s money for 15 years, until his youngest child finishes school. Assuming the payout can earn 5% a year above inflation, the present value is 30,000 × (1 − 1.05⁻¹⁵) ÷ 0.05 = **GH₵ 311,389.74**. If he has GH₵ 40,000 of savings and a workplace death benefit of GH₵ 60,000, he might look for term cover of about 311,390 − 100,000 = **GH₵ 211,390**, rounded to GH₵ 210,000 or so. The assumptions matter as much as the arithmetic.

Third-party motor insurance is compulsory. The National Health Insurance Scheme is funded partly through workers’ SSNIT contributions and a levy.

## Common mistakes

- **Insuring small things** (phones, gadgets, extended warranties) and leaving large risks, such as income or the home, uncovered.
- **Underinsuring**, so the average clause cuts every claim.
- **Not reading the exclusions** until a claim is refused.
- **Hiding information** when applying, which can void the policy.
- **Buying savings-linked life policies** when cheap term cover was what the family needed.
- **Buying from unlicensed sellers**, or paying premiums to agents without getting a policy document.
- **Having no emergency fund**, so even small excesses are a problem.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A policy has a GH₵ 1,000 excess. You claim for damage of GH₵ 6,500. How much does the insurer pay?

<Solution>

6,500 − 1,000 = **GH₵ 5,500** (if the item is fully insured and the claim is covered).

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Which of these is most worth insuring for a family with GH₵ 15,000 of savings: a GH₵ 1,500 phone, the income of the main earner, or a GH₵ 800 television?

<Solution>

**The main earner’s income.** Its loss would be far beyond the family’s savings. The phone and television could be replaced from the emergency fund.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** A GH₵ 3,000 extended warranty for a fridge costs GH₵ 450 for three years. The chance of a breakdown in that time is 10%, and an average repair costs GH₵ 1,200. What is the expected payout, and is it good value?

<Solution>

Expected payout = 0.10 × 1,200 = **GH₵ 120**, against a cost of 450. On average the buyer loses **GH₵ 330**. Poor value for anyone who could pay for a repair.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Contents worth GH₵ 80,000 are insured for GH₵ 50,000. A burglary causes a loss of GH₵ 16,000. With an average clause, what is paid (before any excess)?

<Solution>

16,000 × 50,000 ÷ 80,000 = **GH₵ 10,000**.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Health insurance costs GH₵ 1,800 a year with a GH₵ 200 excess or GH₵ 1,500 with a GH₵ 800 excess. Esi expects about one claim a year. Which is cheaper for her, on that expectation?

<Solution>

Low excess: 1,800 + 200 = **GH₵ 2,000** a year. High excess: 1,500 + 800 = **GH₵ 2,300**. With a claim every year, the **lower excess** is cheaper. Higher excesses pay off only when claims are rare.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Kwame is 26, single, with no dependants and no debts. An agent recommends a savings-linked life policy. What would you ask?

<Solution>

**Who would need the money if he died?** With no dependants, he may need little or no life cover. Then: what are the charges, what does it cost to stop early, and how would its returns compare with saving in a low-cost fund separately?

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** A family needs GH₵ 24,000 a year for 20 years. Using a 4% real return, estimate the life cover needed if they have GH₵ 50,000 of savings.

<Solution>

PV = 24,000 × (1 − 1.04⁻²⁰) ÷ 0.04 = **GH₵ 326,167.83**. Minus savings: about **GH₵ 276,000** of cover.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Why does an insurer ask about health conditions before selling life or health cover, and what happens if an applicant hides one?

<Solution>

People who know they are likely to claim are more keen to buy, so without asking, the insurer’s pool would fill with high-risk customers and premiums would rise for everyone. Questions let it price each person’s risk. If an applicant **hides** a relevant condition, the insurer can usually **refuse the claim** or cancel the policy, leaving the family with nothing when they need it most.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Insurance **pools** risk: many pay a **premium** so the few who suffer are paid. On average it costs more than it pays out.
- **Insure what would ruin you**; **self-insure** small losses from an emergency fund.
- Know the **sum insured, excess, exclusions** and your duty of **disclosure**.
- A higher **excess** lowers the premium; it suits people with savings who rarely claim.
- **Underinsurance** triggers the **average clause**: claims are cut in proportion.
- **Term life insurance** protects dependants cheaply; estimate the need as the present value of lost income minus savings.
- Buy only from **licensed** insurers.
