Crypto is talked about with either wild enthusiasm or flat dismissal, and both make it hard to think clearly. This chapter tries to do neither. By the end you will be able to say what a cryptocurrency is and how it differs from money, shares and bonds; measure how volatile it has been and what that means for anyone holding it; explain what a stablecoin is and the ways it can fail; understand who actually holds your coins and what happens if they are lost or stolen; and recognise the scams that use crypto, which are the way most people in practice lose money with it. This is general education, not advice to buy or avoid anything.

## What a cryptocurrency is

A **cryptocurrency** (or crypto, or virtual asset) is a digital token whose ownership is recorded on a **blockchain**: a shared ledger, like the ledger in <Ref to="fi-double-entry" />, copied across thousands of computers instead of kept by one bank. New entries are added in blocks, and the network’s rules, enforced by cryptography and by everyone checking everyone else, make it extremely hard to change past entries or spend the same coin twice.

**Bitcoin**, launched in 2009, was the first. Its rules fix the maximum number of coins that will ever exist. Thousands of other tokens followed, some built for other uses (Ethereum, for example, can run programs called smart contracts), and many created mainly to be sold.

You hold crypto through a **wallet**, software that stores the private key: a long secret number that proves you own the coins and lets you spend them. Whoever has the private key controls the coins.

## What it is not

- **It is not legal tender** in Ghana or the UK: no one is required to accept it, and prices, wages and taxes are not set in it.
- **It is not a bank deposit.** There is no deposit protection (<Ref to="fi-banking" />) and usually no compensation scheme if something goes wrong.
- **Most crypto produces no cash flow.** A share is a claim on a company’s profits; a bond pays interest; property earns rent. You can value each by its present value (<Ref to="fi-time-value" />). An unbacked token like Bitcoin pays nothing: its price is whatever the next buyer will pay. That does not make it worthless, since gold also pays nothing, but it means there is no anchor for its value, and nothing to stop the price falling a long way if buyers lose interest.

## Volatility

Crypto prices have been far more volatile than shares. Bitcoin, the largest, has fallen by more than 70% from a peak several times, and smaller tokens have often fallen 90% or more, or to nothing.

**Worked example (fictional prices).** A token is bought at 100. It rises to 140, then falls to 70, then 35, then recovers to 60.

- From the peak of 140 to 35 is a **drawdown of 75%** (<Ref to="fi-risk-return" />).
- From 35, it needs to rise **300%** to get back to 140, and 186% just to return to the buyer’s 100.
- At 60, the buyer is down 40%, even after the price has risen 71% from its low.

**Leverage makes it worse.** Some platforms let you trade with borrowed money. With 5 times leverage, GH₵ 1,000 of your own money controls GH₵ 5,000 of crypto. A 20% fall loses GH₵ 1,000, all of your money, and the platform automatically sells your position (**liquidation**). Crypto prices can fall 20% in a day.

## Stablecoins

A **stablecoin** is a token designed to keep a steady value, usually one US dollar. Most claim to hold reserves, such as dollars and US Treasury bills, equal to the coins issued, so each can be swapped back for a dollar.

They are useful for moving money across borders quickly. Their risks:

- **The reserves may not be there**, or may be in assets that cannot be sold quickly. You depend on the issuer’s honesty and on whoever checks its accounts.
- **The issuer can freeze coins** at an address, for example at a regulator’s request.
- **“Algorithmic” stablecoins** with no real reserves can collapse: in May 2022, TerraUSD lost its link to the dollar within days and became almost worthless, wiping out billions of dollars.
- **For someone whose life is in cedis**, a dollar stablecoin is a bet on the dollar against the cedi. If the cedi strengthens, it loses value in cedis.

## Who holds your coins

**Custody** means who controls the private keys.

- **On an exchange or app (custodial).** The platform holds the keys and you have an account balance. It is easy, but you depend on the platform staying solvent, honest and secure. When the large exchange FTX collapsed in November 2022, customers found their money had been used by the firm and could not withdraw it.
- **In your own wallet (self-custody).** You hold the keys, usually backed up as a **seed phrase** of 12 or 24 words. No one can freeze or lose your coins but you. But if you lose the seed phrase, the coins are gone for ever; and anyone who sees it can take everything. **No genuine company or support agent will ever ask for your seed phrase.**

Crypto transactions are also **irreversible**. A bank or card payment sent by mistake or to a fraudster can sometimes be recalled; a crypto transfer cannot.

## Why so many scams use crypto

Irreversible payments, accounts not tied to real names, easy movement across borders, and the excitement of stories about people getting rich make crypto ideal for fraud. It has become one of the commonest ways for investment fraudsters to be paid. The common patterns:

- **Fake trading platforms.** A website or app shows your balance growing fast. The numbers are invented; your deposit was taken on day one.
- **“Mentors” and romance.** A friendly stranger online, often met by a “wrong number” message or on a dating site, builds trust over weeks, then introduces a “sure” investment. (This is sometimes called pig butchering.)
- **Ponzi schemes** that pay early investors with later investors’ money and reward you for recruiting friends.
- **Pump and dump**: promoters hype a new token, sell theirs as the price rises, and leave buyers holding the loss.
- **Fake giveaways** using celebrities’ names: “send 1, get 2 back”.
- **Recovery scams**: after a loss, someone offers to get your money back for a fee. They are the same people, or others targeting victims.

The red flags are those of every investment fraud, sharper: **guaranteed or very high returns**, pressure to act fast, a stranger who found you, requests to pay in crypto, a platform you cannot find on the regulator’s register, and fees or “taxes” demanded before you can withdraw.

## If you decide to buy

Nothing here says crypto will rise or fall. If you choose to hold some, these principles limit the damage:

1. **Only money you could lose entirely** without harming your life, after your emergency fund and debts are dealt with (<Ref to="fi-saving" />, <Ref to="fi-debt" />).
2. **Never borrow** to buy it, and avoid leverage.
3. **Use a provider registered or licensed** by your country’s regulator, and check the register yourself.
4. **Understand custody** before you buy, and keep your seed phrase offline and private.
5. **Treat it as speculation**, a small part of a portfolio, not as savings.
6. **Keep records** of every purchase and sale for tax.

<Note>

**Ghana (checked November 2026).** The Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025 (Act 1154) sets up registration and licensing of crypto businesses, led by the Bank of Ghana with the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Financial Intelligence Centre. Exchanges, wallet providers, advisers and token issuers must register or be licensed. In November 2026 the Bank of Ghana’s virtual assets page still showed its application and fees sections as coming soon, so check its page and the SEC for which providers are registered before using any.

</Note>

<Note>

**United Kingdom (checked November 2026).** The Financial Conduct Authority warns that anyone investing in crypto should be prepared to lose all the money they invest. Most crypto activity is not yet covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme or the Financial Ombudsman Service. Crypto firms must be registered with the FCA for anti-money-laundering purposes, and a fuller regime for crypto firms is being introduced. Crypto derivatives cannot be sold to retail investors. Gains on crypto may be subject to Capital Gains Tax.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Buying because a price has risen fast**, at the top of the excitement.
- **Investing money you will need**, or borrowed money.
- **Using leverage**, which turns a normal day’s fall into a total loss.
- **Keeping everything on one exchange** without asking what happens if it fails.
- **Sharing a seed phrase** with “support”, or storing it as a photo on a phone.
- **Believing guaranteed returns**, and paying “fees” to withdraw.
- **Paying a recovery service** after being scammed.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A token falls from 80 to 20. What is the fall in per cent, and what rise does it need to get back to 80?

<Solution>

20 ÷ 80 − 1 = **−75%**. It needs 80 ÷ 20 − 1 = **300%**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Why can’t you value Bitcoin the way <Ref to="fi-shares" /> valued a share?

<Solution>

The share’s value came from the **present value of future dividends**. Bitcoin pays no dividends, interest or rent, so there are **no cash flows** to discount; its price depends only on what buyers will pay.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Kojo uses 10 times leverage, putting GH₵ 500 into a GH₵ 5,000 position. What price fall wipes out his money?

<Solution>

A loss of 500 on a 5,000 position is 500 ÷ 5,000 = **10%**. A 10% fall, common in crypto, liquidates him.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Ama holds GH₵ 10,000 worth of a dollar stablecoin bought when GH₵ 15 bought a dollar. The cedi strengthens to GH₵ 12 per dollar. What is her holding worth in cedis, assuming the coin keeps its dollar value?

<Solution>

She holds 10,000 ÷ 15 = 666.67 dollars of coin. In cedis now: 666.67 × 12 = **GH₵ 8,000**, a loss of 20% in cedis even though the coin stayed at one dollar.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** A message from “exchange support” asks Esi to confirm her wallet’s 12-word seed phrase to fix a problem. What should she do, and why?

<Solution>

**Refuse and ignore it.** Anyone with the seed phrase controls the coins and can empty the wallet; no genuine support team ever asks for it. She should report the message to the platform through its official site.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Name three red flags in this offer: “Join our crypto trading group. Our AI bot earns 3% a day, guaranteed. Only 10 places left today. Pay in USDT to start.”

<Solution>

Any three of: a **guaranteed** return; an absurdly **high** return (3% a day is over 4,700,000% a year when compounded); **pressure** (“10 places left today”); payment in **crypto**, which cannot be recovered; an unregulated “group”.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Kwame lost GH₵ 20,000 to a fake platform. A week later someone contacts him offering to recover it for a fee of GH₵ 2,000. What is likely happening, and what should he do?

<Solution>

A **recovery scam**, often by the same people or others who buy lists of victims. Genuine recovery is rare and is not sold by strangers who contact you. He should not pay, and should report the original fraud to the police and the financial regulator, keeping all messages and transaction details.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Esi wants to put 5% of a well-diversified portfolio into Bitcoin. Using this chapter and <Ref to="fi-risk-return" />, describe the worst outcome for her portfolio and whether she could live with it.

<Solution>

If Bitcoin went to zero, her portfolio would lose **5%** from that holding; a 75% fall, which has happened before, would cost **3.75%**. If the rest of her portfolio is sound and she has an emergency fund, she can probably survive either, which is the point of keeping speculative holdings **small**. She should also decide her custody, use a registered provider, and not add more after rises.

</Solution>

## Summary

- A **cryptocurrency** is a token recorded on a **blockchain**; whoever holds the private key controls it.
- It is not legal tender or a bank deposit, and most crypto has **no cash flows**, so nothing anchors its value.
- It is extremely **volatile**: falls of 70% or more have happened repeatedly; **leverage** can wipe you out in a day.
- **Stablecoins** depend on reserves and issuers, can fail, and carry dollar–cedi currency risk.
- **Custody**: exchanges can fail; self-custody can be lost; never share a **seed phrase**; transfers are **irreversible**.
- Crypto is the payment of choice for many **scams**: fake platforms, mentors, Ponzi schemes, pump and dumps, giveaways and recovery scams.
- If you buy: only what you can lose, no borrowing, registered providers, small and as speculation.
