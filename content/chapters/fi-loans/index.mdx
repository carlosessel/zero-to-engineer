A loan can buy something useful now: a motorbike for work, equipment for a business, a course that raises your pay. It can also cost far more than it seemed to. By the end of this chapter you will be able to work out a loan’s monthly repayment, build its repayment schedule and see how much of each payment is interest, compare short and long terms, uncover the true rate behind a “flat rate” loan or an upfront fee, and see what paying extra does.

## The parts of a loan

Every loan has a **principal** (the amount borrowed), an **interest rate**, a **term** (how long you have to repay) and a **repayment**: the amount you pay each period, usually each month. Two more things often appear:

- **Fees**: an arrangement fee to set the loan up, late-payment charges, and sometimes a charge for repaying early.
- **Collateral**: something of value the lender can take if you stop paying. A loan backed by collateral is a **secured loan**: a mortgage is secured on a home, a car loan often on the car. A loan without it is an **unsecured loan**; because the lender takes more risk, the rate is usually higher. Some lenders instead ask for a **guarantor**, a person who promises to pay if you do not.

## Reducing-balance loans

Most bank loans, mortgages and car loans are **reducing-balance** (or amortising) loans. Each month:

1. Interest is charged on the balance still owed: balance × monthly rate.
2. Your fixed repayment pays that interest first.
3. The rest of the repayment reduces the balance.

Because the balance shrinks, the interest part of each payment shrinks too, and the part that repays the loan grows. The repayment itself stays the same every month.

The fixed monthly repayment comes from a formula. With principal P, monthly rate r and n months:

repayment = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ)

The small −n means “one divided by (1 + r) to the power n”. You will rarely work it out by hand: spreadsheets have a built-in function for it, shown below.

**Worked example.** Kwame borrows GH₵ 10,000 for 12 months at 24% a year, charged as 2% a month on the reducing balance.

- Repayment: 10,000 × 0.02 ÷ (1 − 1.02⁻¹²) = **GH₵ 945.60** a month (to the nearest pesewa).
- Month 1: interest 0.02 × 10,000 = 200.00; the other 945.60 − 200.00 = 745.60 reduces the balance to 9,254.40.
- Month 2: interest 0.02 × 9,254.40 = 185.09; 760.51 repays the loan; balance 8,493.89.

Here is the whole **repayment schedule** (also called an amortisation table), with interest rounded to the nearest pesewa each month:

| Month | Payment | Interest | Repaid | Balance after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 945.60 | 200.00 | 745.60 | 9,254.40 |
| 2 | 945.60 | 185.09 | 760.51 | 8,493.89 |
| 3 | 945.60 | 169.88 | 775.72 | 7,718.17 |
| 4 | 945.60 | 154.36 | 791.24 | 6,926.93 |
| 5 | 945.60 | 138.54 | 807.06 | 6,119.87 |
| 6 | 945.60 | 122.40 | 823.20 | 5,296.67 |
| 7 | 945.60 | 105.93 | 839.67 | 4,457.00 |
| 8 | 945.60 | 89.14 | 856.46 | 3,600.54 |
| 9 | 945.60 | 72.01 | 873.59 | 2,726.95 |
| 10 | 945.60 | 54.54 | 891.06 | 1,835.89 |
| 11 | 945.60 | 36.72 | 908.88 | 927.01 |
| 12 | 945.55 | 18.54 | 927.01 | 0.00 |

The last payment is 5 pesewas smaller, because rounding the repayment up to 945.60 overpaid slightly each month; lenders adjust the final payment so the balance ends at exactly zero. In total Kwame repays **GH₵ 11,347.15**, so the loan cost him **GH₵ 1,347.15** in interest.

## A longer term: smaller payments, bigger cost

Stretching the same loan over 24 months instead of 12 lowers the repayment to **GH₵ 528.71** a month. That is easier to fit in a budget, but the balance stays high for longer, so more interest is charged. Kwame would repay **GH₵ 12,689.07** in total, **GH₵ 2,689.07** of interest: about twice as much as over 12 months.

| Term | Monthly repayment | Total repaid | Total interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 months | 945.60 | 11,347.15 | 1,347.15 |
| 24 months | 528.71 | 12,689.07 | 2,689.07 |

When you choose a term, ask for both numbers: the repayment (can I afford it every month?) and the total (what does this cost me?). Choose the shortest term whose repayment fits comfortably in your budget, leaving room for emergencies.

## Paying extra

On a reducing-balance loan, any extra payment goes straight to the balance, and the interest on that amount stops. If Kwame takes the 24-month loan but pays GH₵ 200 extra each month (728.71 in all), he clears it in **17 months** and pays **GH₵ 1,807.52** of interest instead of 2,689.07, a saving of GH₵ 881.55. Check first that your lender allows early repayment and whether it charges for it.

## Flat-rate loans: a rate that hides the real cost

Some lenders, including some microfinance companies and informal lenders, quote a **flat rate**. Interest is worked out on the *original* principal for the whole term, as simple interest, even though you repay part of the principal every month.

**Worked example.** A lender offers GH₵ 10,000 for 12 months at “2% a month flat”.

- Interest: 10,000 × 0.02 × 12 = GH₵ 2,400.
- Total to repay: 12,400, in 12 payments of 12,400 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 1,033.33**.

It sounds the same as Kwame’s 2% a month loan, but it costs GH₵ 2,400 instead of 1,347.15. You pay interest on money you have already paid back: by month 12 you owe less than a tenth of the original, but are still being charged on all of it.

What reducing-balance rate gives a payment of 1,033.33? Trying rates in a spreadsheet (or with the `RATE` function) gives about **3.48% a month**, an effective yearly rate of about **50.7%**, against 26.8% for a true 2% a month. As a rough guide, a flat rate costs close to twice the same reducing-balance rate. Always ask: is this rate flat or on the reducing balance, and what is the APR?

## Upfront fees

A fee taken from the loan when it is paid out raises the true cost, because you pay interest on money you never received. If Kwame’s lender charged a 3% arrangement fee taken from the loan, he would receive 9,700 but still repay 945.60 a month on 10,000. The rate that turns 9,700 into twelve payments of 945.60 is about 2.50% a month, an effective **34.5% a year** instead of 26.8%. This is what an APR captures, which is why <Ref to="fi-interest" /> says to compare loans by APR.

## Doing it in a spreadsheet

Every common spreadsheet has functions for loans. Rates are per period, so a monthly loan uses the monthly rate.

```text
=PMT(2%, 12, -10000)              gives 945.60    (the monthly repayment)
=PMT(2%, 24, -10000)              gives 528.71
=RATE(12, -1033.33, 10000)        gives 3.48%     (the true monthly rate of the flat-rate loan)
=RATE(12, -945.60, 9700)          gives 2.50%     (the true monthly rate with a 3% upfront fee)
```

The principal is entered as a negative number in `PMT` (and the payment as negative in `RATE`) because, from your side, money comes in once and goes out each month. To build the schedule, put the balance in one column, interest as `=balance*rate`, repaid as `=payment-interest` and the next balance as `=balance-repaid`, then copy down, using absolute references for the rate and payment as in <Ref to="fi-spreadsheets" />.

<Note>

**Country notes (checked November 2026).** **Ghana:** before taking a loan, ask whether the rate is flat or on the reducing balance, the total amount you will repay, and every fee; get them in writing. Digital and mobile lenders must be licensed by the Bank of Ghana, which publishes notices naming unlicensed digital credit providers. **United Kingdom:** most credit agreements covered by the Consumer Credit Act 1974 can be cancelled within 14 days (a “cooling-off” period), and you have the right to repay early.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by monthly payment alone.** A longer term lowers the payment and raises the total cost; look at both.
- **Taking a flat rate as a reducing-balance rate.** “2% flat” costs about as much as 3.5% on a reducing balance.
- **Forgetting fees.** An upfront fee or compulsory insurance can add several percentage points to the true rate.
- **Borrowing the maximum offered.** The lender’s limit is what it thinks you can repay, not what you need.
- **No room in the budget.** A repayment that only just fits leaves nothing for an emergency, which then needs another loan.
- **Not reading the agreement.** Late fees, early-repayment charges and what happens to collateral are in the document you sign.

## Practice

Use a spreadsheet’s `PMT` function, or the formula, for the repayments.

**1. (warm-up)** A loan of GH₵ 5,000 charges 1.5% a month on the reducing balance. What is the interest in the first month?

<Solution>

0.015 × 5,000 = **GH₵ 75**.

</Solution>

**2. (standard)** What is the monthly repayment on GH₵ 5,000 over 6 months at 1.5% a month?

<Solution>

`=PMT(1.5%, 6, -5000)` = **GH₵ 877.63** (the formula gives 877.626…).

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** For the loan in problem 2, how much of the first payment repays the loan, and what is the balance after it?

<Solution>

Interest 75.00, so 877.63 − 75.00 = **GH₵ 802.63** repays the loan. Balance: 5,000 − 802.63 = **GH₵ 4,197.37**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** GH₵ 20,000 is borrowed over 36 months at 1% a month. What are the repayment and the approximate total repaid?

<Solution>

`=PMT(1%, 36, -20000)` = **GH₵ 664.29** a month. Total ≈ 36 × 664.29 = **GH₵ 23,914.44**, so about GH₵ 3,914 of interest. (The exact total depends on how the last payment is adjusted; the unrounded figure is 23,914.30.)

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** In Kwame’s schedule, why is the interest in month 7 (105.93) about half the interest in month 1 (200.00)?

<Solution>

Interest is 2% of the balance still owed. By month 7 the balance owed at the start of the month has fallen to 5,296.67, about half the original 10,000, so the interest is about half.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** A lender offers GH₵ 3,000 for 6 months at “3% a month flat”. What is the total interest and the monthly payment?

<Solution>

Interest: 3,000 × 0.03 × 6 = **GH₵ 540**. Total 3,540, so 3,540 ÷ 6 = **GH₵ 590** a month.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** What is the true reducing-balance monthly rate of the loan in problem 6? Use `=RATE(6, -590, 3000)`.

<Solution>

About **4.94% a month**, far above the advertised 3%.

</Solution>

**8. (standard)** Kwame can afford GH₵ 600 a month. Which of the two terms in the chapter fits, and what does it cost him compared with the other?

<Solution>

Only the **24-month** term (528.71) fits; 945.60 does not. It costs 2,689.07 − 1,347.15 = **GH₵ 1,341.92** more in interest than the 12-month term.

</Solution>

**9. (stretch)** On the 24-month loan, paying GH₵ 200 extra each month clears it in 17 months with GH₵ 1,807.52 of interest. How much interest does that save, and why does extra payment save so much?

<Solution>

2,689.07 − 1,807.52 = **GH₵ 881.55**. Each extra cedi reduces the balance immediately, so it stops being charged 2% a month for every remaining month of the loan.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** Explain why a 3% arrangement fee taken from Kwame’s loan raises the effective yearly rate from 26.8% to about 34.5%.

<Solution>

He receives only 9,700 but pays the same twelve repayments of 945.60 as if he had received 10,000. Paying the same amount back for less money received means a higher rate: about 2.50% a month instead of 2%, and 1.025¹² − 1 ≈ 34.5% a year.

</Solution>

## Summary

- A loan has a **principal**, rate, **term** and **repayment**, often fees, and sometimes **collateral** (a **secured loan**) or a **guarantor**.
- In a **reducing-balance** loan, interest is charged on what is still owed; each fixed repayment pays the interest first and the rest reduces the balance.
- A **repayment schedule** shows the interest part shrinking and the repaid part growing each month.
- A longer term means smaller repayments and a larger total cost.
- Extra payments cut the balance at once and save interest for the rest of the loan.
- A **flat rate** charges interest on the original principal for the whole term; it costs close to twice the same reducing-balance rate.
- Upfront fees raise the true rate; compare loans by APR and total repaid. In a spreadsheet, `PMT` gives the repayment and `RATE` the true rate.
