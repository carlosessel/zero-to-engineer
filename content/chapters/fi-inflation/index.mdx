A cedi today does not buy what it bought five years ago, and it will not buy as much in five years’ time. Inflation is the reason, and it quietly decides whether your savings, your pay and your investments are really growing. By the end of this chapter you will be able to say how inflation is measured, build a simple price index, work out what inflation does to money kept as cash, and turn any interest rate or return into a **real** one that tells you whether you are actually better off.

## What inflation is

**Inflation** is a general rise in prices over time. “General” matters: one item becoming dearer (tomatoes after a bad harvest) is not inflation on its own, but most things becoming dearer together is. The **inflation rate** is how fast prices rise, usually given as the percentage change over the last 12 months.

The flip side of rising prices is falling **purchasing power**: how much a given amount of money will buy. If prices rise 10%, GH₵ 1,000 buys what GH₵ 909.09 bought a year earlier (1,000 ÷ 1.10), not what GH₵ 900 bought. As in <Ref to="fi-numbers" />, undoing a 10% rise means dividing by 1.10, not taking off 10%.

Two related words:

- **Deflation** is a general fall in prices. It sounds pleasant, but it usually comes with falling wages and people delaying purchases, and it makes debts harder to repay in real terms.
- **Hyperinflation** is extremely fast inflation, often defined as more than 50% a month, when money loses its value so quickly that people stop wanting to hold it.

## How inflation is measured

Statistics offices measure inflation with a **price index**: a number that tracks the cost of a fixed **basket** of goods and services a typical household buys, compared with a starting year called the **base year**, which is given the value 100. The main measure in most countries is the **consumer price index (CPI)**. Each month, staff record the prices of hundreds of items (food, rent, transport, school fees, phone credit) in shops and markets across the country, and weight each one by how much households spend on it.

**Worked example: building an index.** Here is a fictional, much smaller basket for one household for a month, priced in 2025 (the base year) and again in 2026.

| Item | Quantity | 2025 price | 2025 cost | 2026 price | 2026 cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rice, 5 kg bag | 4 | 120 | 480 | 126 | 504 |
| Cooking gas refill | 2 | 150 | 300 | 171 | 342 |
| Transport for the month | 1 | 600 | 600 | 690 | 690 |
| Electricity for the month | 1 | 220 | 220 | 231 | 231 |
| **Basket** | | | **1,600** | | **1,767** |

1. The basket cost GH₵ 1,600 in the base year, so 1,600 is index 100.
2. The same basket cost GH₵ 1,767 in 2026. The index is 1,767 ÷ 1,600 × 100 = **110.44** (to two decimal places).
3. The inflation rate for this basket is 110.44 ÷ 100 − 1 = **10.44%**.

Notice that rice rose only 5% and electricity 5%, but gas rose 14% and transport 15%. The index is an average weighted by spending, so transport, the largest item, pulls it up most.

Between any two years, inflation is the change in the index: if the index goes from 125 to 140, prices rose 140 ÷ 125 − 1 = 12%. Not 15%: the index points are not percentages unless you start from 100.

**Your own inflation is different.** The CPI basket is an average household’s. If you spend more than average on transport and transport prices jump, your personal inflation rate is higher than the published one. The worked example shows how to work out your own.

## What inflation does to cash

Money kept as cash, or in an account that pays no interest, loses purchasing power every year that prices rise. Prices compound like interest, so the damage grows faster than you might expect.

**Worked example.** Prices rise 10% a year for 5 years. Prices end up 1.10⁵ = 1.6105 times as high, an increase of **61.05%**, not 50%. GH₵ 1,000 kept in a drawer is still GH₵ 1,000, but it buys what 1,000 ÷ 1.6105 = **GH₵ 620.92** bought at the start. More than a third of its value has gone without a single cedi being spent.

The rule of 72 from <Ref to="fi-interest" /> works for prices too: at 6% inflation prices double in about 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years (11.9 exactly). At 24% they double in about 3.

## Nominal and real

A **nominal** amount or rate is the one you see quoted, in money of the day. A **real** amount or rate has been adjusted for inflation, so it measures what money can buy.

- Your savings account pays 12% a year: that is a **nominal** interest rate.
- If prices rose 8% over the same year, your money grew 12% but prices grew 8%, so what you can buy grew by less than 12%.

The exact **real return** comes from dividing the multipliers, as in <Ref to="fi-numbers" />:

real return = (1 + nominal rate) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1

**Worked example.** Nominal return 12%, inflation 8%: 1.12 ÷ 1.08 − 1 = 0.0370, a real return of **3.70%**. Saving GH₵ 1,000 gives GH₵ 1,120 a year later, and since prices are 8% higher that buys what 1,120 ÷ 1.08 = GH₵ 1,037.04 bought at the start: 3.70% more.

The shortcut **nominal − inflation** (12% − 8% = 4%) is close when both rates are small, but drifts badly when they are large. With a 5% savings rate and 23% inflation, the shortcut says −18%, but the real return is 1.05 ÷ 1.23 − 1 = **−14.63%**. Use the division whenever inflation is more than a few per cent, which in Ghana it often is.

A **negative real return** means you can buy less at the end than at the start, even though the number in your account went up. This is the trap of “safe” savings during high inflation: the balance grows, but more slowly than prices.

**Pay works the same way.** A 6% pay rise when inflation is 9% is a real pay cut: 1.06 ÷ 1.09 − 1 = −2.75%.

## Comparing money across years

To compare amounts from different years, convert them into the same year’s money with the index: multiply by (index in the new year ÷ index in the old year).

**Worked example.** A bag of sugar cost GH₵ 50 in a year when the index was 160. The index is now 248. In today’s money that is 50 × 248 ÷ 160 = **GH₵ 77.50**. If the bag costs GH₵ 70 today, sugar has actually become cheaper in real terms, even though its price went up.

The same method tells you whether your pay has kept up: a salary of GH₵ 3,000 when the index was 160 needs to be 3,000 × 248 ÷ 160 = GH₵ 4,650 when it is 248 to buy the same.

## Why inflation happens, and what is done about it

Inflation has several causes, often at once: too much money chasing too few goods; rising costs, such as fuel, that businesses pass on; and a falling currency, which makes imports dearer. Expectations matter too: if everyone expects prices to rise, workers ask for more and shops raise prices, which keeps it going.

**Central banks** such as the Bank of Ghana and the Bank of England try to keep inflation low and steady, mainly by setting a **policy rate**, the interest rate that guides the rates banks charge and pay. Raising it makes borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, which slows spending and, over a year or two, inflation. That is why savings rates and loan rates tend to rise and fall with inflation.

## Protecting yourself

None of this is advice for your situation, but these are the general ideas:

- **Compare every rate with inflation.** A savings account, a Treasury bill or an investment is worth having only if its return, after fees and tax, beats inflation over the time you hold it.
- **Don’t hold more cash than you need.** Keep an emergency fund (<Ref to="fi-saving" />), but money for years ahead loses value as cash.
- **Watch real pay.** Ask whether your pay rise beats inflation, and use the index when negotiating.
- **Fixed-rate debt gets lighter.** When inflation is high, a fixed repayment is worth less each year in real terms. But lenders know this, so rates on new loans rise with inflation.
- <Ref to="fi-risk-return" /> and the chapters after it cover the investments that have tended to beat inflation over long periods, and the risks they carry.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring inflation entirely**, and treating a 10% return as 10% richer when prices rose 12%.
- **Subtracting instead of dividing** when inflation is high: 5% − 23% overstates the real loss.
- **Adding inflation rates** over several years instead of compounding them: three years of 10% is 33.1%, not 30%.
- **Reading index points as percentages**: a rise from 125 to 140 is 12%, not 15%.
- **Comparing prices across decades without adjusting**, so everything looks cheaper “in the old days”.
- **Assuming the published rate is your rate.** It is an average; your basket may differ.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Prices rise 20% over a year. What does GH₵ 600 at the end buy, in start-of-year money?

<Solution>

600 ÷ 1.20 = **GH₵ 500**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** A price index rises from 100 to 107.5. What was the inflation rate?

<Solution>

107.5 ÷ 100 − 1 = **7.5%**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** In the basket example, what would the index be if only the transport price had changed (to 690) and everything else had stayed at 2025 prices?

<Solution>

Basket = 480 + 300 + 690 + 220 = 1,690. Index = 1,690 ÷ 1,600 × 100 = **105.63** (to two decimal places). Transport alone added more than half of the 10.44% rise.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A Treasury bill pays 25% a year and inflation is 20%. What is the real return? What does the shortcut give?

<Solution>

1.25 ÷ 1.20 − 1 = **4.17%**. The shortcut gives 25 − 20 = 5%, which overstates it.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Kojo keeps GH₵ 2,000 at home for 3 years while inflation is 20% a year. What is it worth in start-of-period money?

<Solution>

2,000 ÷ 1.20³ = 2,000 ÷ 1.728 = **GH₵ 1,157.41**. It has lost more than 42% of its purchasing power.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Esi’s pay rises from GH₵ 4,000 to GH₵ 4,240 a month while inflation is 9%. Is she better off?

<Solution>

Her pay rose 4,240 ÷ 4,000 − 1 = 6%. Real change: 1.06 ÷ 1.09 − 1 = **−2.75%**. She can buy less than before, so in real terms she has had a pay cut.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** A savings account pays 15% and inflation is 15%. What is the real return, and why might the saver still be worse off?

<Solution>

1.15 ÷ 1.15 − 1 = **0%**: the balance exactly keeps pace with prices. But if the interest is taxed, or there are fees, the return after those is below 15%, so the real return is **negative**.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** A fare was GH₵ 3 when the price index was 150 and is GH₵ 8 now that the index is 330. Has the fare risen faster or slower than prices in general?

<Solution>

In today’s money the old fare is 3 × 330 ÷ 150 = GH₵ 6.60. The fare is now 8, more than 6.60, so it has risen **faster** than prices in general: by 8 ÷ 3 − 1 = 166.7%, against 330 ÷ 150 − 1 = 120% for the index.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Inflation** is a general rise in prices; it reduces the **purchasing power** of money.
- A **price index** tracks the cost of a fixed basket against a base year of 100; the **CPI** is the main one.
- Inflation compounds: 10% a year for 5 years raises prices by 61%, and cash loses more than a third of its value.
- **Real return** = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1; subtracting is only a rough guide when rates are small.
- A **negative real return** means you can buy less, even though your balance grew.
- Convert money between years by multiplying by the ratio of the index values.
- Central banks fight inflation mainly with their **policy rate**.
