Every plan in this course runs through an account: pay arrives in one, bills leave from one, savings sit in one. By the end of this chapter you will know what banks and mobile money providers do with your money, the main kinds of account and payment, how to read a statement and spot a wrong entry, how to work out what an account really costs you, and how much of your money is protected if a bank fails.

## What a bank does

A **bank** takes in money from depositors and lends most of it out to borrowers: people buying homes, businesses buying equipment, governments. It pays depositors a lower interest rate than it charges borrowers, and the difference, plus fees, is how it earns a living.

So the money in your account is not sitting in a vault. The bank owes it to you: to the bank your deposit is a liability, and to you it is an asset. That is why banks are licensed and closely watched by a **central bank** or regulator (the Bank of Ghana in Ghana; the Prudential Regulation Authority, part of the Bank of England, in the UK), which sets rules on how much they must keep safe and how much they can lend.

## Kinds of account

- A **current account** is for everyday money: pay comes in, bills and card payments go out. It usually pays little or no interest and may charge a monthly fee.
- A **savings account** is for money you are keeping. It pays interest, and may limit how often you can withdraw.
- A **fixed deposit** locks money for a set period for a higher rate, as <Ref to="fi-saving" /> described.
- A **mobile money** account (a wallet) holds **electronic money**, or e-money: a balance held by a licensed provider, usually a phone company, that you can send, receive and spend using your phone. In Ghana, as in many countries, the provider must keep the same amount of ordinary money in licensed banks to back the e-money, so that balances can always be paid out. Mobile money reaches people far from any bank branch and is one of the main ways money moves in Ghana and much of Africa.

## Ways to pay

| Method | How it works | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Debit card** | Spends money already in your current account | Card fraud; fees for cash withdrawals at other banks’ machines |
| **Credit card** | Borrows from the card company; you repay later | Interest, often high, if you do not repay the full balance each month (the debt chapters cover this) |
| **Bank transfer** | Moves money between accounts by account number | Sending to the wrong account; transfers are hard to reverse |
| **Mobile money transfer** | Moves e-money between wallets by phone number | A fee on sending or cashing out; fraud using fake messages |
| Cash | Notes and coins | Theft; no record unless you keep one |

A **debit** is money going out of an account, and a **credit** is money coming in. (These words have a more precise meaning in bookkeeping, which a later chapter on double-entry explains; on a bank statement they mean simply out and in.)

## Reading a statement

A **bank statement** (or just statement) lists every movement in an account over a period, starting from the **opening balance** and ending with the **closing balance**. Each line shows a date, a description, an amount in or out, and the **running balance**: the balance after that line.

The habit that matters is checking each line. Most errors and frauds are spotted by someone reading their own statement and asking “what is that?”.

## What an account really costs

Fees are easy to overlook because each one is small. Add up a typical month to see the true cost.

**Worked example.** Yaw makes six mobile money transfers of GH₵ 300 a month and cashes out GH₵ 500 twice. On a fictional fee schedule of 0.5% to send and 1% to cash out, his month costs:

- Transfers: 6 × (0.005 × 300) = 6 × 1.50 = GH₵ 9.00
- Cash-outs: 2 × (0.01 × 500) = 2 × 5.00 = GH₵ 10.00
- **Total: GH₵ 19.00 a month, or 12 × 19 = GH₵ 228 a year.**

A bank account with a flat fee of GH₵ 15 a month and free transfers would cost 12 × 15 = GH₵ 180 a year, less for this pattern of use. For someone who makes only one or two small transfers a month, the mobile money wallet would be cheaper. The answer depends on how you use the account, which is why you work it out rather than guess. Real fee schedules differ by provider and change, so use each provider’s current published charges.

### Sending money across borders

Money sent from another country, a **remittance**, has two costs: the **fee** you see, and the **exchange rate margin** you may not. The **mid-market rate** is the rate halfway between what banks buy and sell a currency for; providers often give you a worse rate and keep the difference.

**Worked example (fictional rates).** Adwoa, in the UK, sends £200 to her mother in Ghana. The fee is £3, and the provider’s rate is GH₵ 15.20 per pound when the mid-market rate is GH₵ 15.60.

- Her mother receives 200 × 15.20 = **GH₵ 3,040**. At the mid-market rate it would have been 200 × 15.60 = GH₵ 3,120.
- The hidden cost is 3,120 − 3,040 = GH₵ 80, which is 80 ÷ 15.60 = £5.13 (to the nearest penny).
- Total cost: 3 + 5.13 = **£8.13, about 4.06% of the £200 sent**.

Compare providers on the amount that arrives, not on the advertised fee.

## Deposit protection

Banks are supervised, but they can still fail. **Deposit protection** (also called deposit insurance) is a scheme, usually set up by law, that pays depositors back up to a limit if a licensed bank fails. Three points matter:

1. The limit is usually **per depositor, per institution**. Money above the limit at one institution is not protected by the scheme, though some may be recovered later from what the failed bank owned.
2. It covers licensed institutions only. A savings scheme that is not licensed is not covered.
3. Spreading savings across institutions, each under the limit, keeps all of it protected.

**Worked example.** Esi has GH₵ 15,000 in one Ghanaian bank. Under the limit in force in November 2026 (GH₵ 6,250 per depositor per bank; see the country note), GH₵ 6,250 is protected and 15,000 − 6,250 = GH₵ 8,750 is not. From 1 January 2027 the limit rises to GH₵ 12,500, leaving 15,000 − 12,500 = GH₵ 2,500 unprotected. Holding GH₵ 7,500 at each of two banks would put all of it under the 2027 limit.

<Note>

**Ghana (checked November 2026).** Deposit protection is run by the Ghana Deposit Protection Corporation (GDPC). The limits in force are GH₵ 6,250 per depositor at a bank and GH₵ 1,250 at a specialised deposit-taking institution (such as a savings and loans company, rural bank or microfinance company). The GDPC has announced new limits from 1 January 2027: GH₵ 12,500 for bank deposits, GH₵ 5,000 for specialised deposit-taking institutions, and GH₵ 2,000 for electronic money (mobile money) holders. Banks, specialised deposit-taking institutions and mobile money providers are licensed by the Bank of Ghana. The 1% electronic transfer levy (E-Levy) on mobile money transfers was abolished in April 2025.

</Note>

<Note>

**United Kingdom (checked November 2026).** The Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) protects deposits up to £120,000 per eligible person per authorised bank, building society or credit union (the limit rose from £85,000 on 1 December 2025). Banks that share a banking licence count as one firm, so check which brands share a licence before spreading money between them.

</Note>

## Keeping accounts safe

- **Never share your PIN, password or one-time code**, with anyone, including someone who says they are from your bank or mobile money provider. Real staff never ask for them.
- **Check before you send.** Confirm the name the system shows for the account or number before you approve a transfer; sending to the wrong number is hard to reverse.
- **Be suspicious of urgent messages** saying money was sent to you by mistake, or that your account will be blocked. They are common frauds.
- **Report a lost phone or card at once**, so the SIM and accounts can be blocked.
- **Read your statement every month** and report anything you do not recognise.

The scams chapter later in the course goes further.

## Choosing an account

Ask of any account: Is the provider licensed? Is the money protected, and up to how much? What does it cost for the way *I* use it? How easily can I get my money? Is it convenient where I live and work? A single person may sensibly use several: a mobile money wallet for daily payments, a bank current account for pay and bills, and a savings account for the emergency fund.

## Common mistakes

- **Not reading statements**, so wrong charges and frauds go unnoticed for months.
- **Comparing providers on the advertised fee**, ignoring cash-out charges or exchange rate margins.
- **Keeping all savings in one institution** above the protection limit, or in an unlicensed scheme with no protection at all.
- **Sharing a PIN or one-time code** with a caller who claims to be from the provider.
- **Confusing a debit card with a credit card.** One spends your money; the other borrows.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A statement opens at GH₵ 2,150. Salary of GH₵ 4,000 comes in and rent of GH₵ 1,200 goes out. What is the balance?

<Solution>

2,150 + 4,000 − 1,200 = **GH₵ 4,950**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Is a card payment at a supermarket a debit or a credit on your current account statement?

<Solution>

A **debit**: money going out of the account.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** A fictional provider charges 0.5% to send money. What does it cost to send GH₵ 300 six times?

<Solution>

0.005 × 300 = 1.50 each; 6 × 1.50 = **GH₵ 9.00**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Yaw’s mobile money costs GH₵ 19 a month; a bank account would cost a flat GH₵ 15 a month. How much would the bank account save him in a year, for the same use?

<Solution>

Mobile money: 12 × 19 = 228. Bank: 12 × 15 = 180. Saving: **GH₵ 48** a year.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Esi has GH₵ 15,000 in one Ghanaian bank. Under the limit in force in November 2026, how much is protected and how much is not?

<Solution>

Protected: **GH₵ 6,250**. Not protected by the scheme: 15,000 − 6,250 = **GH₵ 8,750**.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Under the 2027 Ghanaian limits, how much of GH₵ 4,000 held with a specialised deposit-taking institution is protected?

<Solution>

The 2027 limit for specialised deposit-taking institutions is GH₵ 5,000, which is more than 4,000, so **all GH₵ 4,000** is protected.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** Adwoa sends £200 at GH₵ 15.20 per pound. How much arrives, and how much would have arrived at the mid-market rate of GH₵ 15.60?

<Solution>

200 × 15.20 = **GH₵ 3,040**. At the mid-market rate: 200 × 15.60 = **GH₵ 3,120**. The rate cost her mother GH₵ 80.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Adwoa’s provider charges a £3 fee. What is the total cost of the transfer in pounds, and as a percentage of £200?

<Solution>

The rate margin is GH₵ 80, worth 80 ÷ 15.60 = £5.13 (to the nearest penny). Total: 3 + 5.13 = **£8.13**, which is 8.13 ÷ 200 = **4.06%** (to two decimal places; using the unrounded £5.128… gives 4.064%).

</Solution>

**9. (standard)** A caller says they are from your mobile money provider and need the code just sent to your phone to “stop a fraudulent transfer”. What do you do?

<Solution>

**Refuse and hang up.** Providers never ask for your PIN or one-time code; the code is what the caller needs to take your money. If worried, call the provider yourself on the number printed on their official materials or website.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** In the statement walkthrough, the closing balance is GH₵ 3,327.11 and one payment of GH₵ 89.99 was not recognised. If it is refunded, what will the balance be?

<Solution>

3,327.11 + 89.99 = **GH₵ 3,417.10**, the balance before the unrecognised payment.

</Solution>

## Summary

- A **bank** lends out most of what it takes in; your deposit is money it owes you, which is why banks are licensed and supervised by a **central bank** or regulator.
- **Current accounts** are for everyday money, **savings accounts** for keeping it; **mobile money** holds **electronic money** backed by money in banks.
- A **debit** is money out and a **credit** money in on a statement; check each line from **opening balance** to **closing balance**.
- Work out what an account costs for the way you use it; for a **remittance**, compare the amount that arrives, since the **exchange rate margin** is a hidden fee.
- **Deposit protection** repays depositors up to a limit, per depositor per institution, at licensed institutions only.
- Never share a PIN or one-time code; read your statements every month.
