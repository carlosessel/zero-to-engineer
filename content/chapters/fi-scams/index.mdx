Every good thing in this course can be undone in an afternoon by a convincing fraudster. Scams are not only aimed at the foolish or the old: they are designed by professionals to exploit trust, fear, hope and hurry, and they now use fake websites, cloned firms and even artificial intelligence. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain how Ponzi and pyramid schemes work and why they must collapse, recognise impersonation, investment and payment scams, apply the warning signs, check whether a firm is licensed, and know what to do if you or someone you know has been targeted. This is general education, not legal advice.

## Why scams work

Scams work by pushing you to act **before you think**. Most use some mix of:

- **Trust**: they pretend to be a bank, a regulator, a well-known company, a friend or a famous person, or they spend weeks building a relationship.
- **Greed or hope**: returns far above anything legitimate (<Ref to="fi-risk-return" />).
- **Fear**: “your account has been hacked”, “you owe tax and will be arrested”.
- **Urgency**: “only today”, “last few places”, “act now or lose your money”.
- **Social proof**: others, often your own friends or church members, appear to be making money.
- **Secrecy**: “don’t tell your bank, they don’t understand this kind of investment”.

The single most useful habit is to **stop and check** whenever anyone asks you for money or details, however genuine they seem.

## Ponzi schemes

A **Ponzi scheme** pays its early investors with money from later investors, not from any real business. It looks like a high, steady return, until the new money slows and it collapses.

**Worked example.** A fictional scheme promises 10% a month (over 200% a year: 1.10¹² − 1 = 213.8%). The organisers keep 20% of all new money for themselves. Deposits arrive: 10,000, 15,000, 20,000, 20,000, 15,000, 10,000, 5,000, then nothing.

| Month | New money | Total owed | Paid out (10%) | Cash left | Organisers’ cut so far |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10,000 | 10,000 | 1,000 | 7,000 | 2,000 |
| 2 | 15,000 | 25,000 | 2,500 | 16,500 | 5,000 |
| 3 | 20,000 | 45,000 | 4,500 | 28,000 | 9,000 |
| 4 | 20,000 | 65,000 | 6,500 | 37,500 | 13,000 |
| 5 | 15,000 | 80,000 | 8,000 | 41,500 | 16,000 |
| 6 | 10,000 | 90,000 | 9,000 | 40,500 | 18,000 |
| 7 | 5,000 | 95,000 | 9,500 | 35,000 | 19,000 |
| 8 | 0 | 95,000 | 9,500 | 25,500 | 19,000 |

For months, everyone is paid on time, and happy investors bring in friends. But the cash pile shrinks as soon as new money slows. When rumours spread in month 9 and half of the investors ask for their deposits back (47,500), there is only 25,500. The scheme stops paying. In total, investors put in 95,000, received 50,500 back, and the organisers took 19,000. Most investors lose most of their money, and the early “profits” they received were other people’s deposits.

## Pyramid schemes

A **pyramid scheme** pays people mainly for **recruiting others**, who pay to join, rather than for selling real products. Its arithmetic guarantees failure.

**Worked example.** Each member must recruit 6 new members. One person at the top recruits 6; they recruit 36; then 216, and so on. At the 10th level alone the scheme needs 6¹⁰ = **60,466,176** new members, far more people than live in Ghana. Long before that, recruitment stalls, and everyone at the bottom levels, which is always most of the members, loses what they paid.

Some schemes disguise themselves as product sales, with high joining fees or stock you must buy. If the money comes from recruiting rather than from customers outside the scheme, it is a pyramid.

## Investment fraud

- **Unlicensed schemes** offering high returns from forex, crypto, gold, farming, property or “arbitrage”. In 2018 an unlicensed gold-trading company in Ghana collapsed owing large sums to many thousands of customers.
- **Fake trading platforms** that show invented profits but never let you withdraw (<Ref to="fi-crypto" />).
- **Clone firms**: fraudsters copy the name, address and registration number of a real licensed firm and use a slightly different website, email or phone number.
- **Fake endorsements**: adverts using photos, videos or AI-generated voices of politicians, regulators, presenters or business leaders.
- **Boiler rooms**: high-pressure phone sales of worthless or non-existent shares.
- **Recovery scams**: after a loss, someone offers to recover your money for a fee.

## Impersonation and payment scams

- **Bank or mobile money impersonation**: a call or message “from your bank” or “from MoMo” says your account is at risk and asks you to move money to a “safe account”, approve a payment, or read out a code. Your bank will never ask for your PIN, password or a one-time code.
- **“Wrong transfer” scams**: someone claims to have sent you money by mistake and asks you to send it back; the original “transfer” was fake or will be reversed.
- **SIM swap**: a fraudster takes over your phone number to receive your codes; watch for your phone suddenly losing signal.
- **Fake invoices and changed bank details**: a message, apparently from a supplier, landlord or school, says their account details have changed.
- **Advance fee fraud**: you are promised a prize, loan, job, visa or inheritance, but must first pay a “fee”.
- **Romance scams**: an online partner, never met, eventually needs money, or introduces an “investment”.
- **Phishing**: fake messages and websites that collect your login details.

## The warning signs

Be suspicious of anything that:

1. **Promises high returns with little or no risk**, or “guaranteed” returns.
2. **Pressures you to decide quickly.**
3. **Contacts you out of the blue**, by call, message or social media.
4. **Asks you to pay in crypto, gift cards, or to a personal account**, or to move money to a “safe account”.
5. **Asks for PINs, passwords, codes or your seed phrase.**
6. **Asks you to keep it secret**, even from your bank or family.
7. **Pays you mainly for recruiting others.**
8. **Cannot be found on the regulator’s register**, or whose contact details differ from those on it.
9. **Charges fees or “taxes” before you can withdraw.**

## How to check

- **Find the firm on the regulator’s register yourself**, not through a link the firm gives you (<Ref to="fi-markets" />). Check the licence covers what it is offering.
- **Contact the firm using details from the register**, not from the message or advert, to confirm you are dealing with the real firm.
- **Check the regulator’s warnings** of unlicensed firms.
- **Search the firm’s name with words such as “scam” or “complaint”.**
- **Talk to someone you trust** before paying anything.
- **Ask where the return comes from.** If no one can explain clearly how the money is made, assume it isn’t.

## If it happens to you

1. **Stop paying.** Do not pay any more “fees” to release your money.
2. **Contact your bank or mobile money provider immediately**, using the number on your card or their official site. Quick action can sometimes stop or recover a payment.
3. **Change passwords and PINs**, and secure your phone number.
4. **Report it** to the police and the financial regulator, keeping every message, receipt and transaction detail.
5. **Beware of recovery scams** afterwards.
6. **Tell people.** Shame keeps victims quiet, which helps fraudsters find the next one.

<Note>

**Ghana (checked November 2026).** The Securities and Exchange Commission publishes warnings about unlicensed and fraudulent schemes, and in July 2025 warned of schemes using images and AI-generated voices of public figures, operating as faceless social media accounts, and promising unreasonably high returns in short periods. It names unlicensed entities in public notices and asks the public to confirm any firm’s licence with it before investing. Banks and mobile money providers are licensed by the Bank of Ghana, and insurers by the National Insurance Commission. Report fraud to the police and the relevant regulator.

</Note>

<Note>

**United Kingdom (checked November 2026).** Use the FCA Firm Checker and Warning List to check a firm. Since December 2025 fraud is reported to Report Fraud (reportfraud.police.uk or 0300 123 2040), which replaced Action Fraud; in Scotland, report to Police Scotland on 101. Banks must usually reimburse victims of authorised push payment fraud, up to a limit, if it is reported promptly. Contact your bank straight away.

## Common mistakes

- **Believing you are too careful to be scammed.**
- **Trusting a recommendation** because it came from a friend, family member or church group, who may be a victim too.
- **Checking a firm through details the firm supplied.**
- **Paying “release fees”** to get money back.
- **Staying silent** out of embarrassment.
- **Investing because you received early payouts**, which in a Ponzi scheme are just other people’s money.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A scheme promises 5% a week. What is that over a year, compounded?

<Solution>

1.05⁵² − 1 = **1,164%** a year (more than twelve times your money). Nothing legitimate pays this.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Someone calls saying they are from your bank and asks you to read out the code just sent to your phone. What do you do?

<Solution>

**Refuse and hang up.** Banks never ask for one-time codes. Call your bank on the number on your card to check.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** In the Ponzi example, how much had investors received back by the end of month 8, and where did it come from?

<Solution>

1,000 + 2,500 + 4,500 + 6,500 + 8,000 + 9,000 + 9,500 + 9,500 = **GH₵ 50,500**, all from **other investors’ deposits**. No business ever earned a pesewa.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** In a pyramid where each member recruits 4, how many new members are needed at level 8? What does that tell you?

<Solution>

4⁸ = **65,536** at that level alone, and four times as many at the next. Recruitment must stall well before that, leaving most members, at the bottom, with losses.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** An email from your landlord’s address says their bank account has changed and asks you to pay this month’s rent to a new account. What should you check, and how?

<Solution>

Whether the change is **genuine**: phone or visit the landlord using contact details you **already had**, not those in the email. Changed payment details by email are a common fraud, and the landlord’s email may have been hacked.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** A firm’s website gives a licence number that matches a real licensed firm on the regulator’s register. Is that enough?

<Solution>

**No.** It may be a **clone firm**. Check that the website, email, phone number and address match those on the register, and contact the firm using the register’s details.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** A friend says: “It can’t be a scam. I put in GH₵ 2,000 and I’ve already been paid GH₵ 1,200 back.” How would you respond?

<Solution>

Early payouts are exactly how a **Ponzi scheme** builds trust. The GH₵ 1,200 is likely other investors’ money, and she has still not got her own money back. Ask how the returns are actually earned, check the firm on the regulator’s register, and consider withdrawing what she can, without adding more.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Using the warning signs, rate this message: “Hi dear, I’m a forex trader approved by the Central Bank. Join my VIP group and earn 30% weekly, guaranteed. Only 5 slots left. Send USDT to start.”

<Solution>

It shows at least six warning signs: **unsolicited contact**; **guaranteed**, absurdly **high** returns (30% a week); **false authority** (a central bank does not approve forex traders for this); **urgency** (“5 slots”); **payment in crypto**; and no licence you can check. It is almost certainly a scam.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Scams exploit **trust, hope, fear, urgency, social proof and secrecy**; stop and check before paying.
- **Ponzi schemes** pay old investors with new money and collapse when new money slows.
- **Pyramid schemes** pay for recruitment; the numbers make collapse certain.
- Watch for **unlicensed schemes, fake platforms, clone firms, fake endorsements, impersonation, changed bank details, advance fees, romance and phishing**.
- Warning signs: high or guaranteed returns, pressure, cold contact, crypto or unusual payment, requests for codes, secrecy, recruitment, no licence, fees to withdraw.
- Check the **regulator’s register** yourself; if targeted, **stop paying, call your bank, report it**, and beware recovery scams.
