Every business, from a market stall to a bank, keeps its books the same way, with a system more than five hundred years old. By the end of this chapter you will be able to record a business’s transactions with debits and credits, post them to accounts, check them with a trial balance, and turn the result into an income statement and balance sheet. That is the foundation for reading any company’s accounts later in the course, and for keeping the books of a business of your own.

## The accounting equation

You met the balance sheet’s rule in <Ref to="fi-household-statements" />: assets = liabilities + net worth. For a business, the owner’s share is called **equity** (for a company with shareholders, shareholders’ equity), so the rule is written:

assets = liabilities + equity

This is the **accounting equation**. Equity is what the business would owe its owner if it sold every asset and paid every liability. It grows when the owner puts money in, and when the business makes a profit (income minus expenses).

Every transaction changes at least two things, in a way that keeps the equation true. Buy a machine for cash: one asset down, another up. Borrow money: an asset (cash) up, a liability up. Make a sale for cash: an asset up, and equity up through income. **Double-entry bookkeeping** records both sides of every transaction, every time. Because both sides are always recorded, the books can check themselves.

## Debits and credits

Each account has two sides. By a convention that goes back centuries, the left side is called the **debit** side (Dr) and the right side the **credit** side (Cr). Every transaction is recorded as at least one debit and at least one credit of the same total. Whether a debit increases or decreases an account depends on what kind of account it is:

| Kind of account | Examples | Increase with | Decrease with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset | Bank, equipment, money customers owe | Debit | Credit |
| Expense | Rent, materials, wages | Debit | Credit |
| Liability | Loans, money owed to suppliers | Credit | Debit |
| Equity | Owner’s capital | Credit | Debit |
| Income | Sales | Credit | Debit |

A way to remember it: things the business **has** or **uses up** (assets, expenses) increase on the debit side; where the money **came from** (liabilities, equity, income) increase on the credit side.

These words do not mean “good” and “bad”, and here they mean something different from <Ref to="fi-banking" />, where a credit was money into your account. The bank statement is written from the bank’s side: your deposit is money the bank owes you, a liability in its books, so when it grows, the bank credits it. In your own books, the same deposit is an increase in your asset, a debit.

## Recording transactions

Akosua, the tailor from <Ref to="fi-income" />, starts a business. Here is her first month. For each transaction, decide which two accounts change, and which way.

| # | What happened | Debit | Credit | GH₵ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Akosua puts her savings into the business | Bank (asset up) | Owner’s capital (equity up) | 5,000 |
| 2 | Buys a sewing machine, paying from the bank | Equipment (asset up) | Bank (asset down) | 3,200 |
| 3 | Buys fabric from a supplier, to pay later | Materials (expense up) | Payables (liability up) | 800 |
| 4 | Sells dresses for cash | Bank (asset up) | Sales (income up) | 2,600 |
| 5 | Sews school uniforms; the school will pay later | Receivables (asset up) | Sales (income up) | 900 |
| 6 | Pays the supplier part of what she owes | Payables (liability down) | Bank (asset down) | 500 |
| 7 | Pays the rent on her stall | Rent (expense up) | Bank (asset down) | 400 |
| 8 | The school pays part of what it owes | Bank (asset up) | Receivables (asset down) | 600 |

Two new account names: **payables** is money the business owes to suppliers, a liability; **receivables** is money customers owe the business, an asset. (To keep the example short, all the fabric is treated as used this month, so it is an expense. A real business would count unused fabric as stock, an asset.)

This list, each transaction with its debit, credit and amount, is the **journal**, the book of first entry. Transaction 5 shows an important idea: the sale counts in the month the work was done, even though the cash comes later. Recording income when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, rather than when cash moves, is called **accrual accounting**. Recording only cash movements, as Ama did for her household, is **cash accounting**. Businesses above a small size almost always use accrual accounting, which is why their profit and their cash can differ.

## Posting to the ledger

Next, each line of the journal is copied (**posted**) into the accounts it affects. The collection of all the accounts is the **ledger**. Each account is often drawn as a **T-account**, a T with debits on the left and credits on the right, so you can see its balance at a glance. Here is the bank account:

| Bank: debit (in) | GH₵ | Bank: credit (out) | GH₵ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Owner’s capital | 5,000 | 2 Equipment | 3,200 |
| 4 Sales | 2,600 | 6 Payables | 500 |
| 8 Receivables | 600 | 7 Rent | 400 |
| **Total** | **8,200** | **Total** | **4,100** |

Debits 8,200 minus credits 4,100 leaves a **debit balance of 4,100**: there is GH₵ 4,100 in the business’s bank account.

## The trial balance

When every transaction is posted, list every account’s balance in a **trial balance**: debit balances in one column, credit balances in the other. Because every transaction put equal amounts on each side, the two columns must have the same total.

| Account | Debit (GH₵) | Credit (GH₵) |
|---|---|---|
| Bank | 4,100 | |
| Equipment | 3,200 | |
| Receivables | 300 | |
| Materials | 800 | |
| Rent | 400 | |
| Payables | | 300 |
| Owner’s capital | | 5,000 |
| Sales | | 3,500 |
| **Total** | **8,800** | **8,800** |

The totals agree, so every transaction was recorded with equal debits and credits. A trial balance that does not agree means a posting error: a figure copied wrongly, a debit posted as a credit, or one side left out.

A trial balance that does agree does not prove the books are right. It cannot catch a transaction left out entirely, or one posted to the wrong account on the correct side (rent posted as materials, say). It is a check on the arithmetic, not on judgement.

## From the trial balance to the statements

The trial balance sorts straight into the two main statements.

**Income statement for the month:**

| | GH₵ |
|---|---|
| Sales | 3,500 |
| Less materials | −800 |
| Less rent | −400 |
| **Profit** | **2,300** |

**Balance sheet at the end of the month:**

| Assets | GH₵ | Liabilities and equity | GH₵ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank | 4,100 | Payables | 300 |
| Equipment | 3,200 | Owner’s capital | 5,000 |
| Receivables | 300 | Profit for the month | 2,300 |
| **Total** | **7,600** | **Total** | **7,600** |

Assets of 7,600 = liabilities of 300 + equity of 7,300 (her 5,000 plus the month’s profit). The profit of 2,300 is not the same as the cash: the bank holds 4,100, but 3,200 of her 5,000 went into a machine, 300 of sales are still owed to her, and she still owes 300 for fabric. This is the same lesson as the household cash flow statement, and the reason companies publish a cash flow statement as well.

## A ledger in Python (optional)

This short program holds the journal as a list of (debit account, credit account, amount) and works out every balance. Debits are added and credits subtracted, so a positive balance is a debit balance. If every entry is balanced, all the balances together must add up to zero.

```python
entries = [
    ("Bank", "Owner's capital", 5000),
    ("Equipment", "Bank", 3200),
    ("Materials", "Payables", 800),
    ("Bank", "Sales", 2600),
    ("Receivables", "Sales", 900),
    ("Payables", "Bank", 500),
    ("Rent", "Bank", 400),
    ("Bank", "Receivables", 600),
]

balances = {}
for debit, credit, amount in entries:
    balances[debit] = balances.get(debit, 0) + amount
    balances[credit] = balances.get(credit, 0) - amount

for account, balance in balances.items():
    side = "Dr" if balance > 0 else "Cr"
    print(f"{account:16} {side} {abs(balance):>6,}")

print("Total:", sum(balances.values()))
```

```text
Bank             Dr  4,100
Owner's capital  Cr  5,000
Equipment        Dr  3,200
Materials        Dr    800
Payables         Cr    300
Sales            Cr  3,500
Receivables      Dr    300
Rent             Dr    400
Total: 0
```

Accounting software does exactly this, at scale. Knowing what it is doing lets you check it and read its reports.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading “credit” as good and “debit” as bad.** They are only the two sides; which one increases an account depends on the kind of account.
- **Mixing up the bank’s view and your own.** A deposit is a credit on the bank’s statement and a debit in your books.
- **Recording only one side.** Every transaction needs equal debits and credits, or the trial balance will not agree.
- **Treating an owner’s money as income.** Akosua’s 5,000 is capital (equity), not sales; it is not profit.
- **Counting a sale only when the cash arrives** when keeping accrual accounts. The uniforms were a sale in the month the work was done.
- **Trusting a balanced trial balance completely.** It cannot find a missing transaction or one posted to the wrong account on the right side.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Which side increases each of these: (a) a bank account; (b) a loan; (c) sales; (d) rent expense; (e) owner’s capital?

<Solution>

(a) **Debit** (asset). (b) **Credit** (liability). (c) **Credit** (income). (d) **Debit** (expense). (e) **Credit** (equity).

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** A business borrows GH₵ 10,000 from a bank, paid into its bank account. What are the debit and credit?

<Solution>

**Debit Bank 10,000** (asset up); **credit Loan 10,000** (liability up).

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Akosua pays a helper GH₵ 600 in wages from the bank. Record it.

<Solution>

**Debit Wages 600** (expense up); **credit Bank 600** (asset down).

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A customer pays GH₵ 300 that they already owed for an earlier order. Record it, and say whether it is income this month.

<Solution>

**Debit Bank 300; credit Receivables 300.** It is **not** new income: the sale was recorded when the work was done. One asset (money owed) has turned into another (cash).

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Akosua takes GH₵ 1,000 from the business for her own use. Is it an expense? Record it.

<Solution>

It is **not** an expense of the business; it is the owner taking back some of her equity, called **drawings**. **Debit Drawings 1,000** (which reduces equity), **credit Bank 1,000**.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Find the bank balance from these movements, starting at zero: debits 5,000, 2,600 and 600; credits 3,200, 500 and 400.

<Solution>

Debits 8,200, credits 4,100: a **debit balance of GH₵ 4,100**.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** A trial balance has debits of GH₵ 12,450 and credits of GH₵ 12,540. Suggest what might have gone wrong.

<Solution>

The difference is 90, which is divisible by 9, so a likely cause is **two digits swapped** when copying a figure (such as 450 written as 540; 540 − 450 = 90). It could also be one side of a transaction posted with the wrong amount. Check each posting against the journal.

</Solution>

**8. (standard)** The trial balance agrees, but Akosua finds she forgot to record a GH₵ 150 electricity bill. Why did the trial balance not catch it, and what entry fixes it?

<Solution>

A transaction left out entirely changes neither column, so the totals still agree. The fix: **debit Electricity 150; credit Bank 150** (or credit Payables 150 if not yet paid).

</Solution>

**9. (stretch)** Using the chapter’s trial balance, add transactions 9, a GH₵ 700 cash sale, and 10, GH₵ 250 of fabric bought for cash. What is the new profit, and the new bank balance?

<Solution>

Sales 3,500 + 700 = 4,200. Materials 800 + 250 = 1,050. Profit: 4,200 − 1,050 − 400 = **GH₵ 2,750**. Bank: 4,100 + 700 − 250 = **GH₵ 4,550**.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** Show that the chapter’s balance sheet satisfies the accounting equation, and explain why the profit (2,300) differs from the increase in the bank since Akosua put in her capital (4,100 − 5,000 = −900).

<Solution>

Assets 4,100 + 3,200 + 300 = 7,600; liabilities 300; equity 5,000 + 2,300 = 7,300; 300 + 7,300 = **7,600**. ✓ The bank fell by 900 overall despite a profit of 2,300 because 3,200 of cash went into equipment (an asset, not an expense), 300 of sales have not been paid yet, and 300 of materials have not been paid for yet: 2,300 − 3,200 − 300 + 300 = −900.

</Solution>

## Summary

- The **accounting equation**: assets = liabilities + **equity**. Every transaction keeps it true.
- **Double-entry bookkeeping** records every transaction as equal **debits** and **credits**.
- Assets and expenses increase with a debit; liabilities, equity and income increase with a credit.
- Transactions go first into the **journal**, then are **posted** to accounts in the **ledger**, often drawn as **T-accounts**.
- **Receivables** are owed to the business; **payables** are owed by it. **Accrual accounting** records income and expenses when earned or incurred, not when cash moves.
- A **trial balance** checks that debits equal credits; it cannot catch missing or misplaced transactions.
- The trial balance sorts into the income statement and balance sheet; profit and cash differ.
