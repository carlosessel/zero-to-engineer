Before a big decision, read the official guidance for your country and, if you need it, speak to a qualified adviser who is licensed by your country’s regulator. ## What personal finance is

**Personal finance** is how one person or one household earns, spends, saves, borrows, protects and invests money over their life. It is different from **corporate finance**, which is how companies raise and use money, and from **public finance**, which is how governments do it. You will meet company finance later in this course, because when you buy a share you become a part-owner of a company, and you need to read its accounts to judge it.

Almost every money decision you will ever make is one of seven things:

| Activity | The question it answers | Where in this course |
|---|---|---|
| **Earn** | How much comes in, and what is taken off before I get it? | Income and payslips |
| **Spend** | Where does it go, and is that where I want it to go? | Budgeting |
| **Save** | What am I keeping for later, and where is it kept? | Saving, banking |
| **Borrow** | What does using someone else’s money cost me? | Interest, loans, debt, credit |
| **Protect** | What happens if something goes wrong? | Insurance, scams |
| **Invest** | How can money I will not need for years grow? | The investing parts |
| **Give** | What do I owe or want to share: family, community, tax? | Tax, the financial plan |

None of these is separate from the others. Borrowing at a high cost makes saving harder; an emergency with no savings pushes people into expensive borrowing; investing before you have any savings can force you to sell at a bad time. A large part of this course is about the order to do things in.

## Money moves: a household in one month

Here is a fictional person, Ama, who lives in Accra. After deductions, her **net pay** (the money that actually reaches her account) is GH₵ 4,000 a month. GH₵ is the symbol for the Ghana cedi; examples in this course use cedis, but every idea works in any currency.

| Where it goes | Amount (GH₵) |
|---|---|
| Spending: rent, food, transport, phone, family | 2,800 |
| Paying back a loan | 400 |
| Emergency savings | 500 |
| Investing for the long term | 300 |
| **Total** | **4,000** |

Every cedi has a job. That is the idea behind a **budget**: a plan, made in advance, for where your money will go. You will build one in <Ref to="fi-budgeting" />.

Now look at what one year of this month builds. Over 12 months, Ama pays 12 × 400 = GH₵ 4,800 off her loan, puts 12 × 500 = GH₵ 6,000 into savings, and invests 12 × 300 = GH₵ 3,600 (before any growth or losses). Small monthly amounts, repeated, become large ones. That repetition is the single most important pattern in personal finance, and it works in both directions: a debt growing at a high interest rate builds up the same way.

## Assets, liabilities and net worth

Two words appear in almost every chapter.

An **asset** is something you own that has money value: cash, savings, a phone, a car, shares, land. A **liability** is money you owe: a loan, an unpaid bill, a credit card balance. Your **net worth** is what you would have left if you sold all your assets and paid off all your liabilities:

net worth = assets − liabilities

If Ama has GH₵ 6,500 in savings and a phone worth GH₵ 1,500, her assets are GH₵ 8,000. If she still owes GH₵ 3,000 on her loan, her net worth is 8,000 − 3,000 = GH₵ 5,000. Net worth can be negative, and for many young people with student loans it is. That is not a failure; it is a starting point you can measure. The household accounts chapter turns this into a proper household balance sheet.

## Saving and investing are different

**Saving money** means keeping it safe for later, usually in a bank account or a similar place where its value in cedis does not fall and you can get it back quickly. **Investing** means putting money into something, such as a company’s shares, a government bond or a fund, that you expect to grow over years, while accepting that its value can go down as well as up, and sometimes cannot be sold quickly.

The difference is **risk**: the chance that what you get back is different from what you expected, including the chance of getting back less than you put in. Savings in a sound bank carry little risk. Investments carry more, and in exchange are expected, though never promised, to grow faster over long periods. Anyone who promises high returns with no risk is either mistaken or lying; the chapter on scams and fraud shows how to tell.

A common rule of thumb follows from this: money you will need within the next few years, and an emergency fund, belongs in savings; money you will not need for many years can be invested. You will see why in the chapter on risk and return.

## Why rules and products vary by country

The ideas in this course are the same everywhere: interest is calculated the same way in Kumasi and in Manchester, a balance sheet balances in every currency, and diversification reduces risk in every market. What changes from country to country is:

- **Tax**: rates, allowances, which accounts are tax-free.
- **Pensions**: how much is compulsory, and what your employer must pay.
- **Products**: what kinds of account, fund and loan are sold, and what they are called.
- **Protection**: how much of your deposit is guaranteed if a bank fails, and who you can complain to.
- **Regulators**: the official bodies that license financial firms and can stop fraud.

So this course teaches the ideas in general and puts anything country-specific in a clearly marked country note, like the one below. The notes cover Ghana and the United Kingdom, and say when they were checked. Rules change, often every year in a budget, so always confirm a country note against the official source before you act on it.

<Note>

**Country note (checked November 2026).** Each country has official bodies that license and supervise financial firms. In **Ghana**: the Bank of Ghana (banks, savings and loans companies, mobile money), the Securities and Exchange Commission (investment firms, brokers, collective investment schemes such as unit trusts and mutual funds), the National Pensions Regulatory Authority, the National Insurance Commission, and the Ghana Revenue Authority for tax. In the **UK**: the Financial Conduct Authority (most financial firms, including investment firms and lenders), the Prudential Regulation Authority (banks and insurers), and HM Revenue and Customs for tax. Before giving money to any firm, check that it appears on the right regulator’s official register.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with investing.** Picking shares before you have a budget, an emergency fund and a plan for expensive debt is the most common way beginners lose money they needed.
- **Treating a rule of thumb as a rule.** “Save 20%” or “keep three months of spending” are starting points, not laws. Your situation decides.
- **Copying someone else’s choices.** A friend’s investment that went up tells you nothing about the risk they took or whether it suits you.
- **Trusting a promise instead of a register.** Firms that guarantee high returns, or pressure you to decide today, are the warning signs of fraud. Check the regulator’s register first.
- **Assuming another country’s rules apply.** Tax and pension advice from a UK or US website may be wrong in Ghana, and the other way round.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Sort these into assets and liabilities: a savings account, a phone, a student loan, money a friend owes you, an unpaid electricity bill.

<Solution>

**Assets:** the savings account, the phone, and the money your friend owes you (it is owed *to* you, so it has value to you). **Liabilities:** the student loan and the unpaid electricity bill (you owe both).

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Kofi has GH₵ 2,400 in a bank account and a laptop worth GH₵ 3,100. He owes GH₵ 1,750 on a phone he is paying off. What is his net worth?

<Solution>

Assets: 2,400 + 3,100 = GH₵ 5,500. Liabilities: GH₵ 1,750. Net worth = 5,500 − 1,750 = **GH₵ 3,750**.

</Solution>

**3. (warm-up)** Esi puts GH₵ 250 into savings every month. Ignoring any interest, how much has she saved after two years?

<Solution>

Two years is 24 months, so 24 × 250 = **GH₵ 6,000**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Which of the seven activities (earn, spend, save, borrow, protect, invest, give) does each of these belong to? (a) Paying for health insurance. (b) Buying units in a fund for retirement. (c) Paying income tax. (d) Taking a loan to buy a motorbike.

<Solution>

(a) **Protect**. (b) **Invest**. (c) **Give**: tax is money you owe the government, which the course covers with tax basics; you could also see it as part of earning, since it is taken from your pay. (d) **Borrow**.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Yaw’s net pay is GH₵ 3,200 a month. He spends GH₵ 2,450, pays GH₵ 300 towards a loan and saves the rest. How much does he save each month, and how much over a year?

<Solution>

Each month: 3,200 − 2,450 − 300 = **GH₵ 450**. Over a year: 12 × 450 = **GH₵ 5,400**.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Akua has GH₵ 900 in savings, owes GH₵ 4,500 on a student loan and owns nothing else of value. What is her net worth? Is something wrong?

<Solution>

Net worth = 900 − 4,500 = **−GH₵ 3,600**. Nothing is wrong: a negative net worth is common early in life, especially with a student loan. What matters is the direction it moves each year.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** Someone offers you an investment “with guaranteed returns of 10% a month and no risk”. Using this chapter, name two reasons to be suspicious.

<Solution>

Any two of: higher expected return always comes with more risk, so “no risk” with a high return is a contradiction; “guaranteed” returns from an investment are a classic sign of fraud; 10% a month is far above what sound savings or investments pay; and you have not yet checked whether the firm is on the regulator’s register.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Kwame has GH₵ 5,000 he will need in eight months for school fees, and GH₵ 5,000 he will not need for at least fifteen years. Using the difference between saving and investing, where might each amount belong, and why?

<Solution>

The school-fees money is needed soon and for a fixed purpose, so it belongs in **savings**: it must not fall in value, and he must be able to get it back on time. The fifteen-year money can be **invested**, because over that long a period he has time to wait out falls in value, and investments are expected (not promised) to grow faster than savings. This is general reasoning, not advice: his debts, emergency fund and goals would also matter.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Personal finance** is how a person or household earns, spends, saves, borrows, protects, invests and gives money.
- A **budget** gives every unit of money a job before the month starts.
- Small amounts repeated every month become large amounts over years, for savings and for debts.
- **Net worth** = **assets** − **liabilities**; it can be negative, and its direction matters more than its size.
- **Saving money** keeps it safe and available; **investing** accepts **risk** in exchange for expected, never guaranteed, growth.
- The ideas are universal, but tax, pensions, products, protection and regulators vary by country, so check country notes against official sources.
- This course is education, not personal financial advice.
