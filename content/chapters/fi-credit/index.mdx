When you apply to borrow, the lender wants to know one thing: will this person pay me back? Your past behaviour with credit is the evidence, and it is kept in a record you have the right to see. By the end of this chapter you will know what a credit report holds, what a credit score is and what moves it, how a lender decides, how to build a record from nothing, and how to check and correct yours.

## Credit, credit reports and credit bureaus

**Credit** here means any arrangement where you get money, goods or services now and pay later: a loan, a credit card, a phone on an instalment plan, a mobile loan, sometimes a utility or phone contract.

Lenders share information about how their customers handle credit with **credit bureaus** (in the UK, **credit reference agencies**): companies licensed to collect it and supply it to other lenders. What a bureau holds about you is your **credit report** (also called a credit file). It usually contains:

- **who you are**: name, date of birth, addresses, and identity numbers;
- **your accounts**: each loan, card or credit agreement, the lender, the limit or amount borrowed, and the current balance;
- **payment history**: month by month, whether each payment was on time, late, or missed;
- **defaults and court judgments**: debts the lender has given up on or taken to court;
- **searches**: a record of lenders that have looked at your report when you applied.

A credit report does not usually hold your income, your savings, or your religion, ethnicity or health.

## Credit scores

A **credit score** is a number calculated from your credit report to summarise how likely you are to repay. Each bureau and many lenders have their own scoring system and their own scale, so there is no single “true” score: a number from one bureau cannot be compared directly with a number from another.

The exact formulas are secret, but the same things matter almost everywhere:

1. **Payment history.** Paying on time, every time, matters most. A missed payment, and above all a default, does the most damage, and can stay on a report for years.
2. **How much of your available credit you use.** Your **credit utilisation** is your total card and overdraft balances divided by your total limits. Using a small share suggests you are not stretched.
3. **How long you have had credit.** A longer record of handling credit well gives lenders more evidence.
4. **Recent applications.** Several applications for credit in a short time can suggest you are in difficulty.
5. **Defaults, court judgments and insolvency**, which weigh heavily against you.

**Worked example.** Ama has two credit cards. Card A has a limit of GH₵ 5,000 and a balance of 1,000; card B a limit of 3,000 and a balance of 2,400.

- Card A: 1,000 ÷ 5,000 = 20%. Card B: 2,400 ÷ 3,000 = **80%**.
- Overall: (1,000 + 2,400) ÷ (5,000 + 3,000) = 3,400 ÷ 8,000 = **42.5%**.

Many lenders see high utilisation, and especially a card close to its limit, as a sign of stress. There is no official safe percentage, but lower is better. Paying GH₵ 1,000 off card B would bring her overall utilisation to 2,400 ÷ 8,000 = 30%.

## How a lender decides

A lender’s decision combines your credit report with what you tell it, mainly your income and your existing commitments, to judge whether you can afford the repayments (the debt-to-income ratio from <Ref to="fi-debt" />). Each lender decides for itself, so a refusal from one does not mean a refusal from all. But applying to many in a row adds searches to your report, so find out why you were refused before applying again.

## Building a record from nothing

Someone who has never borrowed has a **thin file**: little or no history, so lenders have no evidence either way, and may refuse or charge more. You build a record by using a small amount of credit and repaying it perfectly:

- keep a bank account in good order, and pay any phone or utility contract on time;
- if you take a small loan or card, borrow only what your budget can repay, and set up an automatic payment so you are never late;
- keep your details (address, phone) up to date with lenders, so reports match you.

Never borrow just to build a score: interest is a real cost, and the record you build only matters if you will need credit later.

## Protecting your record

- **Check your report** from each bureau, especially before a big application and at least once a year. Checking your own report does not harm your score.
- **Dispute mistakes.** If an account is not yours, a payment is wrongly marked late, or a settled debt still shows as owing, contact the bureau and the lender with your evidence. Bureaus must investigate and correct inaccurate information.
- **Watch for identity theft.** An account or search you do not recognise can mean someone has borrowed in your name. Report it at once to the lender, the bureau and the police.
- **Think hard before guaranteeing someone else’s loan.** If they do not pay, you must, and their missed payments can affect you.
- **Never pay anyone who promises to “clean” or “repair” your record.** Only inaccurate information can be removed, and you can dispute it yourself for free.

<Note>

**Country notes (checked November 2026).** **Ghana:** credit bureaus are licensed by the Bank of Ghana under the Credit Reporting Act. The Bank of Ghana’s list of licensed credit bureaus names three: XDS Data Ghana, Dun and Bradstreet Credit Bureau Limited, and MyCredit Score Limited. You can request your report from each and dispute errors with them. **United Kingdom:** the three credit reference agencies are Experian, Equifax and TransUnion. You have a legal right to a free statutory credit report from each, and a lender that refuses you credit after checking your file must tell you so and which agency it used.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Never checking your report**, so an error or a fraudulent account goes unnoticed until you are refused.
- **Missing payments because of a small balance.** A late payment hurts the record whatever the amount; an automatic payment of at least the minimum prevents it.
- **Applying to many lenders after a refusal**, which adds search after search.
- **Running cards close to their limits**, even if you pay on time.
- **Believing one score is the truth.** Scores differ between bureaus and lenders; the report underneath is what matters.
- **Paying for “credit repair”.** Disputing errors is free; accurate negative information cannot be removed by anyone.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A card has a limit of GH₵ 10,000 and a balance of GH₵ 4,500. What is its utilisation?

<Solution>

4,500 ÷ 10,000 = **45%**.

</Solution>

**2. (standard)** Ama’s cards have limits of GH₵ 5,000 and 3,000 and balances of 1,000 and 2,400. What is her overall utilisation, and how much must she pay off to bring it to 30%?

<Solution>

Overall: 3,400 ÷ 8,000 = **42.5%**. 30% of 8,000 is 2,400, so total balances must fall to 2,400: she must pay off 3,400 − 2,400 = **GH₵ 1,000**.

</Solution>

**3. (warm-up)** Which of these are usually in a credit report? Income, payment history, savings balance, defaults, past addresses, searches by lenders.

<Solution>

**Payment history, defaults, past addresses and searches by lenders.** Income and savings balances are usually not; lenders ask for income separately.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Kofi checks his report and finds a loan he never took. What should he do?

<Solution>

Treat it as possible **identity theft**: report it at once to the lender named and to the bureau, dispute the entry with any evidence he has, and report it to the police. Then check his reports from the other bureaus for the same problem.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Esi is refused a loan. Her friend says to apply to five other lenders that week. Why might that be a poor idea, and what should she do first?

<Solution>

Each application can leave a search on her report, and several in a short time can make lenders more wary. She should first find out why she was refused: check her reports for errors or problems, and see whether the loan was affordable on her income.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Rank these by how much they are likely to harm a credit record, most harmful first: one payment five days late; a default; checking your own report; high card utilisation.

<Solution>

**A default** (most), then **one late payment** and **high utilisation** (both harmful; the late payment usually more so, and utilisation falls as soon as balances are paid down), and **checking your own report** does no harm at all.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Yaw, aged 22, has never borrowed and is refused a phone on an instalment plan. Suggest three things that could help him build a record over the next year, and one thing he should not do.

<Solution>

Any three of: keep his bank account in good order; pay any phone or utility contract on time; take a small credit product he can afford and repay it on time by automatic payment; keep his address and details up to date with providers. He should **not** borrow more than he needs, or pay a company to “build” or “repair” his score.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Akosua is asked to be guarantor for her cousin’s GH₵ 8,000 loan. What does that commit her to, and what should she check first?

<Solution>

If her cousin does not pay, **she must**, up to the whole amount, and missed payments may affect her own record. She should check whether she could afford to repay it herself from her budget, read the agreement, and decide whether she would be willing and able to pay it all if needed.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Credit** is anything bought or borrowed now and paid for later; **credit bureaus** (**credit reference agencies**) collect how you handle it.
- A **credit report** holds your identity, accounts, payment history, defaults and searches.
- A **credit score** summarises the report; scales differ between bureaus and lenders.
- Payment history matters most; **credit utilisation**, length of history, recent applications and defaults also count.
- Lenders combine your report with your income and commitments to judge affordability.
- Build a **thin file** slowly with small, affordable credit repaid on time.
- Check your reports, dispute errors for free, watch for identity theft, and never pay for “credit repair”.
