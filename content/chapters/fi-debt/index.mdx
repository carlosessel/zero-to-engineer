Debt is not a moral failing. It is a tool that can help or harm, and many people end up with more of it than they planned. By the end of this chapter you will be able to tell useful debt from harmful debt, measure how much debt your income can carry, recognise predatory lending, see why minimum payments keep you in debt, plan a way out with the avalanche or snowball method, and know where to get free, honest help.

## Useful debt and harmful debt

**Debt** is money you owe. People often divide it into “good” and “bad” debt. A more useful test asks two questions:

1. **What does it pay for?** Borrowing that builds your income or something of lasting value (training that raises your pay, tools for a business, a sensible home loan) can be worth its cost. Borrowing for things that are used up quickly (meals, clothes, a holiday, gifts) leaves the debt behind long after the thing is gone.
2. **What does it cost?** The same purpose can be a reasonable loan at a low rate or a disaster at a high one. Business equipment financed at 5% a month is not “good debt”.

A loan for a sewing machine that lets Akosua take more orders can pay for itself. The same amount borrowed from a mobile app at a high monthly rate to cover a shortfall in her food budget does not: next month the shortfall is bigger, because the repayment comes out of the same budget.

## How much debt can you carry?

Lenders, and you, can measure the load with the **debt-to-income ratio**: monthly debt repayments divided by monthly income.

debt-to-income ratio = monthly debt repayments ÷ monthly income

**Worked example.** Esi’s net pay is GH₵ 4,000. She repays GH₵ 450 a month on a loan and GH₵ 300 on a phone: 750 ÷ 4,000 = **18.75%** of her pay goes to debt.

Lenders use their own limits, often based on gross pay, and they vary. But the warning signs that debt has become a problem are the same everywhere:

- borrowing to pay for food, rent or other debts;
- paying only the minimum, month after month;
- missing payments, or not opening letters and messages about them;
- the total owed going up, not down.

## Predatory lending

**Predatory lending** means lending on terms designed to profit from people who are desperate or poorly informed, rather than to be repaid fairly. Common signs:

- very high rates, often quoted per day, per week or as a flat fee, so that the yearly cost is hidden;
- fees for “rolling over” a loan you cannot repay, so the debt grows while you pay;
- pressure to decide immediately, or to borrow more than you asked for;
- aggressive or humiliating collection, such as threatening calls or messages to your family, friends or employer;
- lending without checking whether you can afford it;
- a lender that is not licensed.

An unlicensed moneylender, often called a **loan shark**, may also use threats. If a lender is not licensed, the law gives you less protection, and in some countries (the UK, for example) a debt from an unlicensed lender cannot legally be enforced. Check the regulator’s register before you borrow (see the country notes below).

## The minimum-payment trap

Credit cards and some other credit let you pay a **minimum payment** each month, often a small percentage of the balance. Paying only that keeps your account in good standing, but it can keep you in debt for years.

**Worked example.** Kojo owes GH₵ 5,000 on a card charging 3% a month. The minimum is 5% of the balance or GH₵ 50, whichever is larger.

- **Paying only the minimum:** because the payment shrinks as the balance shrinks, the debt takes **105 months**, almost nine years, to clear, and costs **GH₵ 6,128.59** in interest, more than he borrowed.
- **Paying a fixed GH₵ 500 a month:** it is cleared in **13 months**, with **GH₵ 1,033.77** of interest.

Both figures come from working the balance through month by month (interest added, then the payment taken off), with amounts rounded to the nearest pesewa. A fixed payment, kept the same as the balance falls, is the single most effective change.

## Planning a way out

The method is the same whatever you owe:

1. **List every debt**: who it is owed to, the balance, the interest rate (monthly or yearly, but all the same way), and the minimum payment.
2. **Stop adding to it.** Put cards away; stop new borrowing while you repay.
3. **Budget a fixed amount for debt each month**, more than the total of the minimums. <Ref to="fi-budgeting" /> shows how to find it.
4. **Always pay every minimum**, so nothing goes into arrears (missed payments that bring fees and damage your credit record).
5. **Put everything extra on one debt at a time.** When it is cleared, add its payment to the next. Which one first is the only choice left.

### Avalanche or snowball

- The **avalanche method** pays extra on the debt with the **highest interest rate** first. It always costs the least interest.
- The **snowball method** pays extra on the debt with the **smallest balance** first. It clears whole debts sooner, which many people find motivating, but usually costs more.

**Worked example.** Ama has three debts and GH₵ 600 a month to repay them:

| Debt | Balance (GH₵) | Rate per month | Minimum (GH₵) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phone loan | 900 | 1% | 60 |
| Credit card | 3,000 | 3.5% | 105 |
| App loan | 2,500 | 5% | 150 |
| **Total** | **6,400** | | **315** |

The minimums come to 315, leaving 600 − 315 = GH₵ 285 extra each month.

- **Avalanche** (app loan, then card, then phone): debt free in **14 months**, with **GH₵ 1,723.06** of interest.
- **Snowball** (phone, then app loan, then card): debt free in **15 months**, with **GH₵ 2,012.74** of interest. Her first debt is gone in month 3 instead of month 7.

The avalanche saves GH₵ 289.68 and a month. Notice the card: its minimum of 105 is exactly its interest (3.5% of 3,000), so while it gets only the minimum its balance does not fall at all.

Either method works if you stick to it, and sticking to it matters more than the choice. Choose the avalanche if you can stay motivated without quick wins; choose the snowball if an early win will keep you going. Whichever you choose, deal with any debt that carries a risk beyond money first: a secured loan where you could lose a home or a vehicle, or one with a lender that harasses you.

### Consolidation

**Debt consolidation** means taking one new loan to pay off several debts. It helps only if the new loan’s APR is lower than the debts it replaces, its total cost (including fees and a longer term) is lower, and you do not run up the old cards or loans again. Otherwise it simply moves the debt, and often makes it longer.

## Talking to lenders and getting help

If you cannot keep up, contact your lenders **before** you miss a payment. Many will agree a lower payment for a while, freeze interest, or arrange a plan, because getting some money back is better for them than none. Keep a record of every call and agreement.

Free, independent debt advice exists in many countries, and it is worth using early. Be wary of firms that charge large fees to “manage” your debts or promise to make them disappear: the free services do the same work.

<Note>

**Country notes (checked November 2026).** **Ghana:** check that any lender is licensed by the Bank of Ghana; digital and mobile lenders must hold a digital credit services licence, and the Bank of Ghana publishes notices naming unlicensed digital credit providers. If a licensed lender treats you unfairly, complain to the lender first, and then to the Bank of Ghana. **United Kingdom:** free debt advice is available from charities including StepChange and Citizens Advice. StepChange lists Breathing Space among its services, a government scheme that can pause most interest, charges and creditor action for a time while you get advice.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Paying only the minimum** on credit that recalculates the minimum each month; the debt can last for years.
- **Borrowing to repay borrowing**, especially from a more expensive lender.
- **Ignoring the problem** until letters and calls pile up; early contact gets better options.
- **Skipping a minimum to pay extra elsewhere**; missed payments bring fees and damage your record.
- **Consolidating without changing habits**, and ending up with the new loan and new debts.
- **Paying a company for advice** that free debt charities give.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Yaw’s net pay is GH₵ 5,200 and his debt repayments are GH₵ 1,500 a month. What is his debt-to-income ratio?

<Solution>

1,500 ÷ 5,200 = 0.2885, so about **28.85%** (to two decimal places).

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Which method pays extra on the highest-rate debt first, and which on the smallest balance first?

<Solution>

**Avalanche**: highest rate first. **Snowball**: smallest balance first.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Ama’s three debts have minimums of 60, 105 and 150, and she has GH₵ 600 a month. How much is left for the extra payment?

<Solution>

600 − (60 + 105 + 150) = 600 − 315 = **GH₵ 285**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Ama’s credit card balance is GH₵ 3,000 at 3.5% a month, and she pays the GH₵ 105 minimum. What happens to the balance, and why?

<Solution>

It stays at **GH₵ 3,000**. The month’s interest is 0.035 × 3,000 = 105, and the minimum pays exactly that, so nothing is left to reduce the balance.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Order these debts for the avalanche and for the snowball: a family loan of GH₵ 2,000 at 0%; a card of GH₵ 1,500 at 3% a month; an app loan of GH₵ 400 at 6% a month; a store account of GH₵ 2,500 at 2% a month.

<Solution>

**Avalanche** (highest rate first): app loan (6%), card (3%), store account (2%), family loan (0%). **Snowball** (smallest balance first): app loan (400), card (1,500), family loan (2,000), store account (2,500). Here both start with the app loan.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Kojo owes GH₵ 5,000 at 3% a month. Paying only the minimum takes 105 months and costs GH₵ 6,128.59 of interest; a fixed GH₵ 500 a month takes 13 months and GH₵ 1,033.77. How much interest does the fixed payment save?

<Solution>

6,128.59 − 1,033.77 = **GH₵ 5,094.82**.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** A loan advertises “only GH₵ 2 a day for every GH₵ 100 borrowed”. What is that as a simple rate per 30-day month and per 365-day year?

<Solution>

GH₵ 2 on 100 is 2% a day. Per 30 days: 30 × 2% = **60%**. Per year: 365 × 2% = **730%**, before any compounding or fees. Quoting per day hides an enormous cost.

</Solution>

**8. (standard)** A consolidation loan offers to replace Ama’s three debts with one loan of GH₵ 6,400 at 3% a month over 36 months. Should she take it?

<Solution>

Probably **not**. 3% a month is lower than the app loan but much higher than the phone loan, and stretching the debt to 36 months adds many months of interest: `=PMT(3%, 36, -6400)` is about GH₵ 293.14 a month, a total of about 36 × 293.14 = GH₵ 10,553.04, against GH₵ 6,400 + 1,723.06 = 8,123.06 with the avalanche plan. A lower payment is not a lower cost.

</Solution>

**9. (stretch)** Under the snowball method, Ama clears the phone loan in month 3. Where does its GH₵ 60 minimum go from month 4, and why does that matter?

<Solution>

It is added to the payment on the next debt in the order, the app loan, together with the extra. Money freed by each cleared debt “snowballs” onto the next, so payments on the remaining debts grow over time.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** List three signs in this chapter that a lender may be predatory, and what you would check before borrowing.

<Solution>

Any three of: rates quoted per day, week or as a flat fee; rollover fees; pressure to decide now or borrow more; threatening or shaming collection; no check of whether you can afford it; no licence. Before borrowing, check the lender is on the regulator’s register and get the APR, the total repayable and every fee in writing.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Debt** is useful when it builds income or lasting value at a cost below its benefit, and harmful when it pays for things used up quickly, especially at high rates.
- The **debt-to-income ratio** (repayments ÷ income) measures the load; borrowing to pay for essentials or other debts is a warning sign.
- **Predatory lending** hides the cost, adds rollover fees, pressures and shames; check the lender is licensed.
- Paying only a shrinking **minimum payment** can stretch a debt over many years; a fixed payment clears it far faster.
- List debts, stop borrowing, pay every minimum, and put all the extra on one debt: the **avalanche method** (highest rate) saves the most, the **snowball method** (smallest balance) gives quicker wins.
- **Debt consolidation** helps only if the total cost falls and the old debts stay closed.
- Talk to lenders early, and use free debt advice.
