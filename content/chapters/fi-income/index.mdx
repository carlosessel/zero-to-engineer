Every plan you make with money starts from one number: what actually reaches you. By the end of this chapter you will be able to read a payslip line by line, work out income tax that is charged in bands, tell your marginal rate from your effective rate, convert pay between hours, weeks, months and years, and plan with an income that changes from month to month.

## Gross pay, deductions and net pay

Your **gross pay** is the total you earn before anything is taken off. For an employee it is usually a **basic salary**, the fixed core of the pay agreed in your contract, plus any **allowances**: extra fixed payments for particular costs, such as transport or rent.

**Deductions** are amounts taken off your gross pay before it reaches you. The common ones are income tax, a compulsory social security or pension contribution, and sometimes loan repayments, union dues or a staff welfare fund. What is left is your **net pay**, also called take-home pay:

net pay = gross pay − deductions

Your employer takes the deductions and pays them to the right place for you. The tax part of this system is called **PAYE**, short for Pay As You Earn: income tax collected from each payment of wages, rather than in one lump at the end of the year. Ghana and the UK both use the name.

## Income tax in bands

Most countries do not charge one tax rate on all of your income. They split income into **tax bands**: slices, each with its own rate. The first slice is often taxed at 0%, the next at a low rate, and higher slices at higher rates. Each rate applies only to the income inside its own band. This is called a **graduated** (or progressive) income tax.

The income that tax is worked out on is your **chargeable income** (in the UK, **taxable income**): your gross pay minus anything the law lets you take off first, such as a compulsory pension contribution.

**Worked example.** Kwesi is a fictional employee in Ghana. His basic salary is GH₵ 5,000 a month, and he gets a transport allowance of GH₵ 400.

1. **Gross pay**: 5,000 + 400 = GH₵ 5,400.
2. **Social security**: in Ghana an employee pays 5.5% of basic salary to SSNIT (see the country note below): 0.055 × 5,000 = GH₵ 275. This is taken off before tax is worked out.
3. **Chargeable income**: 5,400 − 275 = GH₵ 5,125.
4. **Tax, band by band**, using Ghana’s monthly bands for 2026:

| Band | Income in this band (GH₵) | Rate | Tax (GH₵) |
|---|---|---|---|
| First 588 | 588.00 | 0% | 0.00 |
| Next 80 | 80.00 | 5% | 4.00 |
| Next 100 | 100.00 | 10% | 10.00 |
| Next 2,900 | 2,900.00 | 17.5% | 507.50 |
| Next 16,000 | 1,457.00 | 25% | 364.25 |
| **Total** | **5,125.00** | | **885.75** |

The first four bands are full: 588 + 80 + 100 + 2,900 = 3,668. That leaves 5,125 − 3,668 = 1,457 in the 25% band, which is not full.

5. **Net pay**: 5,400 − 275 − 885.75 = **GH₵ 4,239.25**.

## Marginal rate and effective rate

Two different “tax rates” describe Kwesi’s position, and both are useful.

- His **marginal rate** is the rate on his *next* cedi of income: the rate of the highest band he reaches. For Kwesi that is **25%**. It tells you how much of a pay rise you keep.
- His **effective rate** (or average rate) is his total tax divided by his gross pay: 885.75 ÷ 5,400 = **16.40%**. It tells you how much of all your pay goes in tax.

The effective rate is always lower than the marginal rate when the lower bands are taxed less, because the lower slices of income are taxed at the lower rates.

### A pay rise never lowers your take-home pay

A common fear is that a rise that “pushes you into a higher band” will leave you worse off. With banded tax it cannot, because the higher rate applies only to the income above the band’s start.

Suppose Kwesi’s basic salary rises by GH₵ 500 to GH₵ 5,500. His gross pay becomes 5,900, SSNIT becomes 0.055 × 5,500 = 302.50, chargeable income becomes 5,597.50 and tax becomes 1,003.88 (to the nearest pesewa). His net pay is 5,900 − 302.50 − 1,003.88 = GH₵ 4,593.62, which is GH₵ 354.37 more than before. Of the GH₵ 500 rise, 27.50 went in SSNIT and 118.13 in tax (25% of the 472.50 rise in chargeable income), and he keeps the rest.

## Reading a payslip

A **payslip** is the statement your employer gives you each pay day showing how your pay was worked out. Layouts differ, but most show the same blocks. Here is Kwesi’s, laid out as a typical payslip would be:

| Section | Line | GH₵ |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings | Basic salary | 5,000.00 |
| | Transport allowance | 400.00 |
| | **Gross pay** | **5,400.00** |
| Deductions | SSNIT (employee, 5.5% of basic) | 275.00 |
| | Income tax (PAYE) | 885.75 |
| | **Total deductions** | **1,160.75** |
| | **Net pay** | **4,239.25** |
| Employer contributions (not deducted from you) | SSNIT (employer, 13% of basic) | 650.00 |

Check every payslip in three ways: do the earnings add up to the gross pay; do the deductions add up to the total deductions; and is gross pay minus total deductions equal to the net pay that arrived in your account? Mistakes happen, and you are the person most likely to notice them.

The last line shows money your employer pays on top of your salary, which never appears in your net pay but builds your pension. Kwesi costs his employer 5,400 + 650 = GH₵ 6,050 a month.

## Converting between hours, weeks, months and years

Pay is quoted in different periods. There are 52 weeks and 12 months in a year, so a month is not four weeks (it is 52 ÷ 12 = 4.33 weeks, to two decimal places).

- **Hourly to monthly**: GH₵ 25 an hour for 40 hours a week is 25 × 40 = 1,000 a week, or 1,000 × 52 = 52,000 a year, or 52,000 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 4,333.33** a month.
- **Weekly to yearly and monthly**: GH₵ 900 a week is 900 × 52 = GH₵ 46,800 a year, or 46,800 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 3,900** a month.
- **Yearly to monthly**: GH₵ 60,000 a year is 60,000 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 5,000** a month.

Always convert through the year: multiplying weekly pay by 4 to get a month understates it by about 8%.

## Irregular income

Traders, farmers, drivers, freelancers and anyone paid by the job have an income that changes from month to month. Being **self-employed** (working for yourself rather than an employer) also means nobody deducts tax for you: you must set it aside and pay it yourself.

**Worked example.** Akosua is a fictional tailor. Her income over six months was GH₵ 2,800, 4,100, 1,900, 3,600, 5,200 and 2,400.

- Total: GH₵ 20,000. Mean: 20,000 ÷ 6 = GH₵ 3,333.33 a month.
- Lowest month: GH₵ 1,900.

Planning her spending on the mean would leave her short in three of the six months. Three habits make irregular income manageable:

1. **Plan essential spending on a low month**, not the average. Akosua’s rent, food and transport must fit within about GH₵ 1,900.
2. **Pay yourself a steady amount.** Put all income into one account, and move a fixed “salary” from it each month. Good months refill it; bad months draw it down.
3. **Set tax aside as you earn.** If she puts 15% of every payment aside, she has 0.15 × 20,000 = GH₵ 3,000 ready after six months. The right percentage depends on her country’s rules; 15% here is only for the example.

## Checking with Python (optional)

If you know a little Python, here is banded tax as a short program. It walks through the bands, taxes the part of the income in each one, and stops when the income runs out. `None` marks the top band, which has no upper limit.

It uses Python’s `decimal` module rather than ordinary numbers. Ordinary Python numbers (called floats) are stored in binary and cannot hold most decimal fractions exactly. Worked out with floats, the tax in problem 3 below comes to 515.7249999999999 instead of 515.725, and rounding that gives 515.72. Payroll rounds a half pesewa up, to 515.73, and `Decimal` with `ROUND_HALF_UP` does the same.

```python
from decimal import Decimal, ROUND_HALF_UP

bands = [(588, "0"), (80, "0.05"), (100, "0.10"), (2900, "0.175"),
         (16000, "0.25"), (30332, "0.30"), (None, "0.35")]

def tax(chargeable):
    total = Decimal("0")
    left = Decimal(str(chargeable))
    for size, rate in bands:
        part = left if size is None else min(left, Decimal(size))
        total += part * Decimal(rate)
        left -= part
        if left <= 0:
            break
    return total.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP)

print(tax(5125))
print(tax(5597.50))
print(tax(3635))
```

```text
1003.88
515.73
```

## Country notes

<Note>

**Ghana (checked November 2026).** The Ghana Revenue Authority’s monthly income tax bands for residents, in force from 1 September 2026, are: first GH₵ 588 at 0%, next 80 at 5%, next 100 at 10%, next 2,900 at 17.5%, next 16,000 at 25%, next 30,332 at 30%, and above GH₵ 50,000 at 35%. The annual bands are the same multiplied by twelve. The employee’s SSNIT contribution of 5.5% of basic salary is taken off before tax; allowances such as transport and rent are added to salary for PAYE. Employers pay a further 13% of basic salary. Of the 18.5% total, 13.5% goes to SSNIT’s first-tier pension and 5% to a privately managed second-tier scheme; a third tier (provident funds) is voluntary. Bonuses and overtime have their own rules. Rates change, often in a budget, so check the GRA’s PAYE page before relying on these.

</Note>

<Note>

**United Kingdom (checked November 2026).** For the 2026 to 2027 tax year (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027), the standard Personal Allowance is £12,570 a year at 0%; the basic rate is 20% on taxable income from £12,571 to £50,270, the higher rate 40% from £50,271 to £125,140, and the additional rate 45% above that. Scotland has different bands. The allowance shrinks by £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000. Employees also pay National Insurance: 8% on pay between £1,048 and £4,189 a month, and 2% above. Employers must give you a payslip. Taking £30,000 a year as an example, income tax is (30,000 − 12,570) × 20% = £3,486.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Planning on gross pay.** Only net pay can be spent. A job offer of GH₵ 5,400 a month was, for Kwesi, GH₵ 4,239.25 in his account.
- **Applying the top rate to all income.** Kwesi’s tax is not 25% of 5,125 (which would be 1,281.25); each band has its own rate.
- **Fearing a pay rise.** A rise into a higher band is taxed at the higher rate only on the extra; you always keep some of it.
- **Converting weekly pay by multiplying by four.** A month is about 4.33 weeks; go through the year.
- **Spending the good months.** With irregular income, plan essentials on a low month and set tax aside from every payment.
- **Never checking payslips.** Wrong tax codes, missed allowances and missing pension contributions happen; check the three totals every month.

**12. (stretch)** Kwesi’s basic salary rises from GH₵ 5,000 to GH₵ 5,500 (allowance unchanged). By how much does his net pay rise, and where does the rest of the GH₵ 500 go?

## Summary

- **Gross pay** is **basic salary** plus **allowances**; **net pay** is what is left after **deductions**.
- **PAYE** collects income tax from each pay packet.
- Income tax is charged in **tax bands** on **chargeable income**; each rate applies only to the income in its band.
- The **marginal rate** is the rate on your next unit of income; the **effective rate** is total tax ÷ gross pay.
- A pay rise always increases net pay, even when it crosses into a higher band.
- Check every **payslip**: earnings add to gross, deductions add up, and gross − deductions = net.
- Convert pay through the year (52 weeks, 12 months).
- With irregular or **self-employed** income, plan essentials on a low month, pay yourself a steady amount, and set tax aside from every payment.
