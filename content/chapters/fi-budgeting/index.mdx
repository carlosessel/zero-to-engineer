A budget is the tool that turns “I don’t know where my money goes” into a plan you chose. By the end of this chapter you will be able to track a month of spending, sort it into categories, turn yearly costs into monthly ones, build a budget with three different methods, run it through a month, and compare what you planned with what happened.

## Step one: find out where the money goes

You cannot plan spending you have not measured. Most people, asked to guess their monthly spending, guess too low, because small, frequent payments are easy to forget.

For one month, record every payment: cash, card, mobile money, transfers. Keep the date, the amount and what it was for. Your bank and mobile money statements cover most of it; a note on your phone covers cash. Then sort each payment into a category, a group of similar spending such as food, transport or rent.

As you sort, notice two things about each category:

- Is it fixed (the same every month, such as rent or a loan repayment) or variable (it changes, such as food or transport)?
- Is it a **need** (something you must pay to live and work: housing, basic food, transport to work, minimum debt repayments) or a **want** (something you choose, which makes life better but could be cut: eating out, entertainment, new clothes beyond the basics)?

Needs and wants are not always clear-cut. A phone is a need for most workers; the most expensive data bundle may not be. Supporting family is a duty in many households and is best treated as a need you plan for, not an afterthought. Decide honestly, and write your reasoning down.

### Worked example: Ama’s tracked month

Ama, from <Ref to="fi-the-map" />, has net pay of GH₵ 4,000. She thought she was saving GH₵ 800 a month. After tracking every payment for a month, she sorted them:

| Category | Need or want | Fixed or variable | Spent (GH₵) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent | Need | Fixed | 1,200 |
| Food and groceries | Need | Variable | 750 |
| Transport | Need | Variable | 380 |
| Electricity and water | Need | Variable | 180 |
| Phone and data | Need | Fixed | 120 |
| Loan repayment | Need | Fixed | 400 |
| Family support | Need | Fixed | 300 |
| Eating out | Want | Variable | 250 |
| Entertainment | Want | Variable | 150 |
| Clothes | Want | Variable | 150 |
| **Total** | | | **3,880** |

Her needs come to GH₵ 3,330 and her wants to GH₵ 550. That leaves 4,000 − 3,880 = GH₵ 120, not 800, and the 120 was never actually saved: it sat in her account and was spent on small things she did not record. Over a year, an unplanned GH₵ 120 a month is 12 × 120 = GH₵ 1,440 that simply disappears. This is the most common finding when people first track their spending, and it is not a failure: it is the information a budget needs.

## Yearly and irregular costs

Some costs do not come every month: school fees each term, an insurance premium, a festival, a vehicle licence. They wreck a monthly budget unless you plan for them. The fix is to turn each one into a monthly amount and set that amount aside every month in a separate pot, sometimes called a **sinking fund**: money saved steadily for a known future cost.

**Worked example.** School fees are GH₵ 2,700 a term, three terms a year: 3 × 2,700 = GH₵ 8,100 a year, or 8,100 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 675 a month**. Put 675 aside every month and each term’s fees are waiting when they are due.

## Three ways to build a budget

A **budget** is a plan, made before the month starts, for where each part of your income will go. There are many methods; here are three widely used ones, each tried on Ama.

### 1. The 50/30/20 rule

Split net pay into three parts: **50% for needs, 30% for wants, 20% for saving and paying off debt** beyond the minimum. For Ama’s GH₵ 4,000 that is 2,000 for needs, 1,200 for wants and 800 for saving.

It is quick, and it is a useful first check. But it was made popular in countries where rents and food take a smaller share of pay. Ama’s needs are 3,330 ÷ 4,000 = **83.25%** of her pay. No amount of cutting wants will bring that to 50%. For many people in many countries, the percentages are a target to move towards over years, not a rule for this month.

### 2. Zero-based budgeting

In a **zero-based budget**, you give every unit of income a job until nothing is left unassigned:

income − everything assigned = 0

Saving is one of the jobs, assigned on purpose, not whatever happens to be left over. Here is Ama’s zero-based plan for next month. She keeps every need, trims food by planning meals (750 to 700) and moves to a cheaper data bundle (120 to 100), cuts her wants, and assigns GH₵ 400 to savings before the month begins:

| Category | Plan (GH₵) |
|---|---|
| Rent | 1,200 |
| Food and groceries | 700 |
| Transport | 380 |
| Electricity and water | 180 |
| Phone and data | 100 |
| Loan repayment | 400 |
| Family support | 300 |
| Eating out | 150 |
| Entertainment | 100 |
| Clothes | 90 |
| Savings | 400 |
| **Total** | **4,000** |

Everything is assigned: 4,000 − 4,000 = 0. Her savings go from an accidental 120 that vanished to a planned **400, which is 10% of her pay**. That is less than the 800 she imagined, but it is real.

### 3. Envelopes

The **envelope method** gives each variable category its own container, and when a container is empty, spending in that category stops until next month. Traditionally the containers were paper envelopes of cash. Today the same idea works with separate mobile money wallets, separate bank accounts or “pots” inside a banking app, or a spreadsheet that tracks what is left in each category.

Envelopes suit the variable categories (food, transport, eating out, entertainment, clothes), where overspending happens. Fixed costs are paid on pay day.

The three methods work together. Many people use 50/30/20 as a rough check, a zero-based plan to assign every cedi, and envelopes to stick to it during the month.

## Running the budget through a month

A budget is only useful if you check it while the month is happening. Two rules make it work:

1. **Pay yourself first.** Move the savings out on pay day, before spending starts, so that saving is not whatever happens to be left over at the end of the month.
2. **When a category runs out, move money, don’t borrow.** If eating out is overspent, take the money from another want, and accept spending less there. The total stays the same.

Here is Ama’s month. On pay day she pays the fixed costs and moves GH₵ 400 to savings, leaving GH₵ 1,420 for the five variable categories.

At the end of the month, the GH₵ 175 left in her envelopes went to savings, so she saved 575 that month instead of 400. Leftovers at the end of a month are a bonus. Shortfalls are a signal: if one category runs out every month, the plan is wrong, and next month’s budget should change.

## Budget against actual

At the end of the month, compare what you planned with what happened. The difference for each category is its **variance**: budget − actual. A negative variance means you spent more than planned.

In a spreadsheet (see <Ref to="fi-spreadsheets" />) this is a small table: a column for the budget, one for what was actually spent, and a formula for the difference.

```text
     A            B             C             D
1    Category     Budget        Actual        Difference
2    Rent         1200          1200          =B2-C2
3    Food         700           812           =B3-C3
4    Transport    380           355           =B4-C4
5    Eating out   150           210           =B5-C5
6    Total        =SUM(B2:B5)   =SUM(C2:C5)   =SUM(D2:D5)
```

The Difference column shows:

```text
Rent          0
Food       -112
Transport    25
Eating out  -60
Total      -147
```

Food was GH₵ 112 over, which is 112 ÷ 700 = 16% over budget; transport was 25 under. Overall this person spent GH₵ 2,577 against a budget of GH₵ 2,430, GH₵ 147 more than planned. The question to ask is *why*: a one-off (a guest staying for a week) needs no change, but a category that is over every month needs a bigger budget, taken from somewhere else.

## Common mistakes

- **Budgeting without tracking first.** A plan built on guesses usually leaves out the small, frequent spending that adds up.
- **Saving only what is left over.** Leftovers vanish. Assign savings first and move it on pay day.
- **Forgetting irregular costs.** Fees, insurance and festivals each need a monthly amount set aside, or they arrive as an emergency.
- **Treating 50/30/20 as a law.** Where needs are 70% or more of pay, the rule cannot be met yet; use it as a direction, not a verdict.
- **Giving up after one bad month.** Overspending is information. Move money between categories, and change the plan if the same category overruns again.
- **A budget so tight it breaks.** Leave a small amount for wants. A plan with no room for life is abandoned within weeks.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Using the 50/30/20 rule, how should a net pay of GH₵ 3,600 be split?

<Solution>

Needs: 0.5 × 3,600 = **GH₵ 1,800**. Wants: 0.3 × 3,600 = **GH₵ 1,080**. Saving and extra debt repayment: 0.2 × 3,600 = **GH₵ 720**. (Check: 1,800 + 1,080 + 720 = 3,600.)

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Sort these into needs and wants for a typical worker: rent, a streaming subscription, bus fares to work, a minimum loan repayment, a new phone when the old one works, basic groceries.

<Solution>

**Needs:** rent, bus fares to work, the minimum loan repayment, basic groceries. **Wants:** the streaming subscription, the new phone while the old one still works.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** School fees are GH₵ 2,700 a term, three terms a year. How much should be set aside each month?

<Solution>

3 × 2,700 = 8,100 a year; 8,100 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 675** a month.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A car insurance premium of GH₵ 1,440 is paid once a year. What is the monthly sinking fund amount?

<Solution>

1,440 ÷ 12 = **GH₵ 120** a month.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Ebo’s net pay is GH₵ 3,200. His zero-based plan assigns GH₵ 2,950 so far. How much is still to be assigned, and what should he do with it?

<Solution>

3,200 − 2,950 = **GH₵ 250**. In a zero-based budget it must be given a job too: savings, a sinking fund, extra debt repayment, or a category that needs more.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Food was budgeted at GH₵ 700 and GH₵ 812 was spent. What is the variance, and by what percentage was the budget exceeded?

<Solution>

Variance: 700 − 812 = **−GH₵ 112**. As a percentage of the budget: 112 ÷ 700 = 0.16, so **16% over**.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** What percentage of Ama’s GH₵ 4,000 went on needs (GH₵ 3,330) and on wants (GH₵ 550) in her tracked month?

<Solution>

Needs: 3,330 ÷ 4,000 = **83.25%**. Wants: 550 ÷ 4,000 = **13.75%**. The remaining 3% (GH₵ 120) was unassigned.

</Solution>

**8. (standard)** Ama moves from a GH₵ 120 to a GH₵ 100 data bundle. How much does she save in a year?

<Solution>

20 a month × 12 = **GH₵ 240** a year.

</Solution>

**9. (standard)** Ama saves GH₵ 400 of her GH₵ 4,000. What is her savings rate, and how much more would she need to save to reach 15%?

<Solution>

400 ÷ 4,000 = **10%**. 15% is 0.15 × 4,000 = 600, so she needs **GH₵ 200** more a month.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** Someone spends GH₵ 15 a day on snacks and drinks. How much is that a year? What share of a GH₵ 4,000 monthly pay is it?

<Solution>

15 × 365 = **GH₵ 5,475** a year. A year’s pay is 12 × 4,000 = 48,000, so it is 5,475 ÷ 48,000 = 11.41% (to two decimal places), more than Ama’s whole savings rate.

</Solution>

**11. (stretch)** In Ama’s month (the walkthrough), eating out overran by GH₵ 60. She covered it from clothes. Why is that better than using her credit card or borrowing from a friend?

<Solution>

Moving money between categories keeps total spending at what she earns, so nothing is carried into next month. Borrowing means next month starts GH₵ 60 down (more with interest), which squeezes the next budget and can repeat until it becomes debt.

</Solution>

**12. (stretch)** Ama saves GH₵ 400 a month for six months as an emergency fund. Her essential spending (the needs in her plan) is GH₵ 3,260 a month. How many months of essentials would the fund cover?

<Solution>

6 × 400 = GH₵ 2,400. 2,400 ÷ 3,260 = 0.74 (to two decimal places), so **about three-quarters of a month**. <Ref to="fi-saving" /> looks at how big an emergency fund should be.

</Solution>

## Summary

- Track every payment for a month before you budget; sort it into categories, fixed or variable, **need** or **want**.
- Turn yearly and irregular costs into monthly amounts in a **sinking fund**.
- The **50/30/20 rule** is a quick check, not a law; where needs exceed 50%, use it as a direction.
- A **zero-based budget** gives every unit of income a job, including saving, until nothing is unassigned.
- The **envelope method** limits each variable category to its own amount; mobile money wallets and banking pots can be envelopes.
- Pay yourself first; when a category runs out, move money between categories rather than borrow.
- Compare budget and actual each month; the **variance** shows which plans need to change.
