Before you invest in anything, it helps to know where investments are bought and sold, who stands between you and the market, how a price is actually set, and who is supposed to keep it all honest. By the end of this chapter you will be able to name the main financial markets, tell a company raising money from investors trading with each other, follow an order from your broker to the exchange and back, read the prices at which you can buy and sell, and check whether a firm offering to invest your money is licensed.

## What financial markets are for

A **financial market** is any place, physical or electronic, where people buy and sell financial assets such as shares, bonds and currencies. Markets do three jobs:

1. **They connect savers with those who need money.** A company wanting to build a factory, or a government building roads, raises money from savers, who expect a return in exchange.
2. **They let you sell.** An investor who could never get their money back until a company closed would be very reluctant to invest. Markets let you sell to someone else. How easily you can do that, quickly and without pushing the price down, is called **liquidity**.
3. **They set prices.** Thousands of buyers and sellers, each with their own information and views, settle on a price, which tells everyone what an asset is currently thought to be worth.

## The main markets

| Market | What is traded | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Money market** | Short-term borrowing, usually under a year | Government Treasury bills |
| **Bond market** | Longer-term loans to governments and companies | A 5-year government bond |
| **Stock market** (equity market) | **Shares**: small pieces of ownership in companies | Shares in a listed bank |
| **Foreign exchange market** | Currencies | Cedis for US dollars |
| **Commodity market** | Raw materials | Cocoa, gold, oil |

Shares and bonds have their own chapters, <Ref to="fi-shares" /> and <Ref to="fi-cash-bonds" />. For now, the key difference: a **share** makes you a part-owner, sharing in profits and losses; a **bond** makes you a lender, owed interest and repayment. There are also markets for **derivatives**, contracts whose value depends on another asset; they are mainly for professionals and are not needed for ordinary investing.

## Primary and secondary markets

When a company or government sells new shares or bonds to raise money, it does so in the **primary market**. The money goes to the issuer.

**Worked example.** Volta Foods, a fictional company, sells 10 million new shares to the public at GH₵ 2.50 each in an **initial public offering** (IPO), the first time its shares are offered to the public. It raises 10,000,000 × 2.50 = **GH₵ 25 million**, which it spends on new equipment. Its shares are then **listed** on the stock exchange.

After that, investors trade the shares with each other in the **secondary market**. If Ama buys 1,000 Volta shares from Kojo next year, Kojo gets the money, not Volta Foods. The company’s share price rising or falling does not change the money it raised, though a high price makes it easier to raise more later.

Most of what people call “the stock market” is the secondary market.

## Who does what

- An **exchange** is the organised market where listed shares and bonds are traded, with rules about who may trade and what companies must publish. Examples: the Ghana Stock Exchange (GSE) and the London Stock Exchange.
- A **broker** is a licensed firm that places orders on the exchange for you; you cannot trade on an exchange directly. On the GSE brokers are called **licensed dealing members**.
- A **central securities depository** (CSD) keeps the official electronic record of who owns which shares and bonds, so you do not need paper certificates.
- A **custodian** holds investments safely on behalf of investors, often for investment funds.
- A **fund manager** invests pooled money for many people, for example in a unit trust or mutual fund.
- A **regulator** licenses and supervises all of these, as the last section explains.

## How a trade happens

1. You open an account with a licensed broker, which also opens your account at the CSD. You prove your identity, as with a bank.
2. You pay money into the account and place an **order**: what to buy or sell, how many, and at what price.
3. The broker sends the order to the exchange, where it waits in the **order book** until it is matched with an opposite order.
4. When it is matched, a **trade** has happened at an agreed price.
5. A few working days later the trade **settles**: the shares move to your account at the CSD, and the money moves to the seller.

## How prices are set

The order book lists everyone waiting to buy, called **bids**, and everyone waiting to sell, called **asks** (or offers), each with a price and a quantity. Here is a fictional order book for Volta Foods shares, in cedis:

| Bids: buyers waiting | | Asks: sellers waiting | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quantity** | **Price** | **Price** | **Quantity** |
| 400 | 3.95 | 4.00 | 300 |
| 1,000 | 3.90 | 4.05 | 500 |
| 600 | 3.85 | 4.10 | 800 |

- The **best bid**, 3.95, is the most anyone will pay right now. The **best ask**, 4.00, is the least anyone will sell for.
- The gap between them, 0.05, is the **bid-ask spread**. As a share of the midpoint (3.975) it is 1.26%. If you bought at 4.00 and immediately sold at 3.95, you would lose that spread. Busy, liquid shares have narrow spreads; rarely traded ones have wide spreads.
- No trade happens while every buyer wants to pay less than every seller wants. A new order that crosses the gap makes a trade.

There are two main kinds of order:

- A **market order** says “buy (or sell) now at the best price available”. It is sure to trade, if there is anyone on the other side, but the price is not guaranteed.
- A **limit order** says “buy at this price or lower” (or “sell at this price or higher”). The price is guaranteed, but it may not trade at all.

**Worked example.** Ama places a limit order to buy 600 Volta shares at up to GH₵ 4.05.

1. The cheapest seller has 300 at 4.00. She buys all of them: 300 × 4.00 = GH₵ 1,200.
2. She still wants 300. The next seller has 500 at 4.05, within her limit. She buys 300: 300 × 4.05 = GH₵ 1,215. That seller has 200 left.
3. In total she pays 1,200 + 1,215 = **GH₵ 2,415** for 600 shares, an average of **GH₵ 4.025** a share, before fees.

The **last traded price**, the price usually quoted in the news, is now 4.05, even though most shares have just changed hands at 4.00. The best ask is now 4.05, so the spread has widened to 0.10.

A price rises when buyers are keener than sellers, and falls when sellers are keener. Nobody “sets” it; it is the result of all the orders.

## Measuring companies and markets

A company’s **market capitalisation** (market cap) is its share price times the number of shares: what the market currently values the whole company at. If Volta Foods has 120 million shares at GH₵ 4.05, its market cap is 120,000,000 × 4.05 = **GH₵ 486 million**.

A **market index** is a single number tracking the prices of a group of shares, so you can see how the market as a whole is doing. If an index moves from 4,000 to 4,300, the shares in it have risen 4,300 ÷ 4,000 − 1 = 7.5% on average, weighted as the index specifies. Examples are the GSE Composite Index (GSE-CI) in Ghana and the FTSE 100 in the UK. <Ref to="fi-funds" /> shows why funds that simply copy an index are popular.

## What trading costs

Every trade has costs on top of the price: the broker’s **commission**, exchange and regulator fees, sometimes taxes, and the spread. They are small per trade, but they eat into returns, especially if you trade often.

**Worked example.** Suppose total charges are 2% of the value on each purchase and each sale (a fictional figure; check your broker’s actual charges). Kojo buys 1,000 shares at GH₵ 4.00 and pays 4,000 × 1.02 = **GH₵ 4,080**. He later sells at GH₵ 4.30 and receives 4,300 × 0.98 = **GH₵ 4,214**. The price rose by GH₵ 300 in total, but he gained only 4,214 − 4,080 = **GH₵ 134**. Just to break even he needed a price of 4.00 × 1.02 ÷ 0.98 = GH₵ 4.17 (rounded up), 4.1% above what he paid.

Costs are covered fully in a later chapter.

## What regulators do and do not do

A **regulator** is the official body that oversees financial markets. Its main jobs:

- **Licensing** brokers, fund managers, exchanges and others, and checking that they are fit to handle other people’s money.
- **Requiring disclosure**: listed companies must publish accounts and important news, so all investors have the same information.
- **Policing misconduct** such as **insider dealing** (trading on important news that has not been made public) and market manipulation (spreading false rumours or faking trades to move a price).
- **Requiring firms to keep clients’ money and investments separate** from their own, so they are not lost if the firm fails.
- **Handling complaints and warning the public** about unlicensed firms.

What a regulator does **not** do is protect you from prices falling. A licensed broker can sell you shares that lose half their value; that is investment risk, not misconduct. Compensation schemes, where they exist, cover a licensed firm failing or behaving wrongly, not a bad investment.

The single most useful check you can make: **before giving money to anyone offering investments, confirm on the regulator’s own website that they are licensed** for that activity. Firms promising high, guaranteed returns from “trading” are a common sign of fraud; a later chapter covers scams in detail.

<Note>

**Ghana (checked November 2026).** The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) regulates the securities markets and licenses brokers, fund managers, custodians and investment advisers; its online register listed 34 licensed broker-dealers in November 2026. The Ghana Stock Exchange lists its licensed dealing members on its website, and shares are held at the Central Securities Depository. The Bank of Ghana regulates banks, and government Treasury bills are sold at regular auctions and can be bought through banks and licensed dealers.

</Note>

<Note>

**United Kingdom (checked November 2026).** The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) authorises and supervises investment firms; check the FCA Register before using one. If an authorised investment firm fails and cannot return your money or investments, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) can pay up to £85,000 per person per firm, but it does not cover losses from poor investment performance.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking the company gets your money** when you buy shares on an exchange. It doesn’t; the seller does.
- **Using market orders in thinly traded shares**, where the next seller may be asking far more than the last price.
- **Treating the last traded price as the price you will get.** You buy at the ask and sell at the bid.
- **Forgetting costs**, which can turn a small price rise into a loss.
- **Believing regulation means safety from loss.** It protects against misconduct and failure, not falling prices.
- **Investing through a firm without checking its licence**, often because a friend or social media recommended it.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** The best bid is GH₵ 1.20 and the best ask GH₵ 1.25. What is the spread, and at what price could you buy immediately?

<Solution>

Spread = 1.25 − 1.20 = **GH₵ 0.05**. You could buy at the ask, **GH₵ 1.25**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** A company sells new shares in an IPO, and a year later you buy some on the exchange. Which purchase is in the primary market, and who received your money?

<Solution>

The IPO is the **primary market**, and the company received the money. Your purchase a year later is in the **secondary market**, and the money went to the investor who sold to you.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Using the original order book above, Kojo places a **market order to sell** 500 Volta shares. What does he receive before fees, and at what average price?

<Solution>

He sells to the best bids: 400 at 3.95 = 1,580, then 100 at 3.90 = 390. Total **GH₵ 1,970**, an average of 1,970 ÷ 500 = **GH₵ 3.94**, below the best bid because his order was bigger than the first buyer’s.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** In the same book, Esi places a limit order to buy 200 shares at GH₵ 3.98. What happens?

<Solution>

The cheapest seller wants 4.00, above her limit of 3.98, so **nothing trades**. Her order joins the bids as the new best bid (200 at 3.98), and the spread narrows to 0.02.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** A company has 50 million shares priced at GH₵ 2.40. What is its market capitalisation?

<Solution>

50,000,000 × 2.40 = **GH₵ 120 million**.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** With charges of 2% on each purchase and each sale, Ama buys 600 shares at GH₵ 4.025 on average and sells them at GH₵ 4.20. What is her gain?

<Solution>

Cost: 600 × 4.025 × 1.02 = 2,415 × 1.02 = 2,463.30. Proceeds: 600 × 4.20 × 0.98 = 2,520 × 0.98 = 2,469.60. Gain = **GH₵ 6.30**, almost nothing, although the price rose 4.3%.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** A message on social media offers “licensed forex trading, 10% a month guaranteed”. Name three checks you would make, and say why the offer itself is a warning sign.

<Solution>

Check the firm’s name on the **regulator’s register** (in Ghana the SEC; in the UK the FCA), check whether the regulator has issued a **warning** about it, and find out where your money would be **held** and who would hold it. The offer itself is a warning sign because no honest investment can **guarantee** a high return: 10% a month compounds to over 200% a year (1.10¹² = 3.14), far beyond anything legitimate, and returns that high always carry high risk.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Why do rarely traded shares tend to have wide bid-ask spreads, and what does that mean for someone who might need to sell quickly?

<Solution>

With few buyers and sellers, there are few orders near the last price, so the best bid and best ask are far apart. Someone who must sell quickly has to accept the best bid, which may be well below the last traded price, and a large sale may have to go down through several bids, lowering the price further. **Low liquidity is a cost and a risk** that the quoted price does not show.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Financial markets** connect savers with borrowers, let investors sell (**liquidity**), and set prices.
- The main markets: money, bond, stock, foreign exchange and commodity markets.
- New shares and bonds are sold in the **primary market** (the money goes to the issuer); investors then trade in the **secondary market**.
- Orders go through a licensed **broker** to an **exchange**; ownership is recorded at a **central securities depository**.
- Prices come from the **order book**: you buy at the **ask**, sell at the **bid**, and the gap is the **bid-ask spread**. **Market orders** are sure to trade; **limit orders** fix the price.
- **Market capitalisation** = price × shares; a **market index** tracks a group of shares.
- **Regulators** license firms and police misconduct, but do not protect you from falling prices. Always check a firm’s licence.
