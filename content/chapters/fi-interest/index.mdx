Interest is the price of money over time. It is what your savings earn and what your debts cost, and the way it is calculated decides whether a sum grows slowly or explodes. By the end of this chapter you will be able to calculate simple and compound interest, see why compounding makes such a large difference over long periods, compare rates quoted per month, per year and with different compounding, read an APR and an AER, and estimate doubling times in your head.

## Interest from zero

When you lend money, whether to a bank by depositing it or to a friend, the borrower usually pays you for the use of it. That payment is **interest**. When you borrow, you pay it. Four words describe any interest arrangement:

- the **principal**: the amount lent or borrowed to begin with;
- the **interest rate**: the percentage charged or paid per period, usually per year;
- the **term**: how long the money is lent for;
- the **balance**: what is owed (or saved) at any moment, principal plus any interest added so far.

## Simple interest

With **simple interest**, interest is worked out on the original principal only, the same amount every period:

interest = principal × rate × number of periods

**Worked example.** Kofi lends GH₵ 2,000 at 12% a year simple interest for 3 years.

1. Interest each year: 0.12 × 2,000 = GH₵ 240.
2. Over 3 years: 3 × 240 = **GH₵ 720**.
3. He gets back 2,000 + 720 = **GH₵ 2,720**.

Simple interest is used for some short loans, some bonds that pay interest out rather than adding it, and, as <Ref to="fi-loans" /> shows, as a misleading way of advertising some loans.

## Compound interest

With **compound interest**, each period’s interest is added to the balance, and the next period’s interest is worked out on that bigger balance. You earn (or pay) interest on interest. This is the **compounding** you met in <Ref to="fi-numbers" />, and it uses the same multiplier:

balance after n periods = principal × (1 + rate)ⁿ

**Worked example.** The same GH₵ 2,000 at 12% a year, compounded yearly, for 3 years:

| Year | Start (GH₵) | Interest at 12% (GH₵) | End (GH₵) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,000.00 | 240.00 | 2,240.00 |
| 2 | 2,240.00 | 268.80 | 2,508.80 |
| 3 | 2,508.80 | 301.06 | 2,809.86 |

Check with the formula: 2,000 × 1.12³ = 2,000 × 1.404928 = **GH₵ 2,809.86** (to the nearest pesewa). Compound interest paid GH₵ 809.86, against GH₵ 720 simple. The difference, GH₵ 89.86, is interest earned on interest.

### Why compounding matters so much over time

Over a few years the difference is modest. Over decades it is enormous, because each year’s growth is bigger than the last. Here is GH₵ 1,000 at 10% a year:

| After | Simple (GH₵) | Compound (GH₵) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | 1,100.00 | 1,100.00 |
| 3 years | 1,300.00 | 1,331.00 |
| 6 years | 1,600.00 | 1,771.56 |
| 30 years | 4,000.00 | 17,449.40 |

After 30 years, simple interest has quadrupled the money; compound interest has multiplied it more than seventeen times. This is why starting to save early matters more than saving a lot later, and why a debt left to compound can grow out of control.

### Compounding works against you on debt

The same arithmetic applies to what you owe. Suppose Yaw borrows GH₵ 2,000 from a lender charging 5% a month, and makes no payments for a year. The balance grows to 2,000 × 1.05¹² = **GH₵ 3,591.71**. He now owes almost twice what he borrowed. Monthly rates that sound small are very large yearly rates, as the next section shows.

## Rates per month, per year, and compounding frequency

Interest can be added (compounded) yearly, quarterly, monthly or even daily. A rate is usually quoted per year, then divided by the number of periods. A yearly rate of 12% compounded monthly means 12% ÷ 12 = 1% a month, added each month.

Because each addition earns interest in later periods, more frequent compounding gives a slightly bigger result. For 12% a year:

| Compounded | Rate per period | Growth over a year | Effective yearly rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yearly | 12% | 1.12 | 12.00% |
| Quarterly | 3% | 1.03⁴ = 1.1255 | 12.55% |
| Monthly | 1% | 1.01¹² = 1.1268 | 12.68% |
| Daily | 12% ÷ 365 | 1.1275 | 12.75% |

The **nominal rate** is the headline yearly rate before compounding (12% here). The **effective annual rate** is what you actually earn or pay over a year once compounding is included:

effective annual rate = (1 + nominal rate ÷ periods per year)^(periods per year) − 1

To compare two products fairly, compare their effective annual rates.

## APR and AER

Two standard figures exist so that you can compare like with like:

- The **APR** (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing, including interest *and* compulsory fees such as an arrangement fee, expressed as one yearly percentage. Use it to compare loans and credit cards: the lower the APR, the cheaper the borrowing, for the same amount and term.
- The **AER** (annual equivalent rate, called APY, annual percentage yield, in some countries) is the effective yearly rate on savings, including compounding. Use it to compare savings accounts: the higher the better.

A loan quoted at “3% a month” is far more than 36% a year once compounded: 1.03¹² − 1 = 42.58%. And if there is a fee as well, the APR is higher still. When a lender quotes only a monthly rate, or only a fee, ask for the APR. A lender that will not tell you is a lender to avoid.

<Note>

**Country notes (checked November 2026).** **United Kingdom:** credit adverts that give an interest rate or cost must show a representative APR, and savings accounts quote the AER. **Ghana:** short loans from mobile apps and some microfinance lenders are often priced per month or as a flat fee. Ask for the yearly rate and the total amount you will repay, and check that a digital lender is licensed: the Bank of Ghana publishes notices naming unlicensed digital credit providers.

</Note>

## The rule of 72

How long does money take to double? There is a quick estimate:

years to double ≈ 72 ÷ yearly interest rate (as a number, not a decimal)

- At 8%: 72 ÷ 8 = **about 9 years**. (Exactly: 1.08⁹ = 1.999, so 9 years.)
- At 12%: 72 ÷ 12 = **about 6 years**. (Exactly 6.12 years.)
- At 36%, a common credit card or loan rate: 72 ÷ 36 = **about 2 years**. An unpaid debt at 36% doubles in roughly two years (exactly 2.25).

It is an approximation that works best for rates between about 5% and 15%, and is close enough for a decision at the table.

## Saving regularly

Most people save a little each month rather than one lump. Each payment then compounds for a different length of time. If you add GH₵ 1,000 at the end of each year to an account earning 10% a year:

- End of year 1: 1,000.
- End of year 2: 1,000 × 1.10 + 1,000 = 2,100.
- End of year 3: 2,100 × 1.10 + 1,000 = **3,310**.

You paid in 3,000 and earned 310 of interest. Saving GH₵ 500 at the end of each month at 1% a month for a year in the same way gives GH₵ 6,341.25, of which 6,000 is your money and 341.25 interest. The chapter on the time value of money gives a single formula for this; a spreadsheet does it in a column, as in <Ref to="fi-spreadsheets" />.

## Checking with Python (optional)

This prints GH₵ 1,000 at 10% a year under simple and compound interest, year by year.

```python
principal = 1000
rate = 0.10
for year in range(0, 7):
    simple = principal + principal * rate * year
    compound = principal * (1 + rate) ** year
    print(year, f"{simple:,.2f}", f"{compound:,.2f}")
```

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying a monthly rate by 12.** 3% a month is 42.58% a year compounded, not 36%.
- **Comparing loans on the interest rate alone.** Fees change the true cost; compare APRs, and the total you will repay.
- **Comparing savings on the nominal rate.** Compare AERs, which include compounding.
- **Waiting to start saving.** The early years compound for longest; GH₵ 1,000 at 10% is 17,449 after 30 years but only 6,727 after 20 (1.1²⁰ = 6.7275).
- **Ignoring a small debt.** At high monthly rates an unpaid balance doubles quickly; the rule of 72 tells you how quickly.
- **Using the rule of 72 for precise answers.** It is an estimate; use the formula when the exact figure matters.

**5. (standard)** A savings account pays 18% a year compounded monthly. What is its effective annual rate (its AER)?

<Solution>

Monthly rate 18% ÷ 12 = 1.5%. 1.015¹² = 1.1956 (to four decimal places), so the AER is **19.56%**.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** A lender charges 4% a month, compounded monthly. What is that as an effective yearly rate?

<Solution>

1.04¹² = 1.6010 (to four decimal places), so **60.10%** a year, far more than 4 × 12 = 48%.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** Use the rule of 72 to estimate how long money takes to double at 6% a year. How close is it?

<Solution>

72 ÷ 6 = **12 years**. Check: 1.06¹² = 2.0122, so after 12 years it has just over doubled. Very close.

</Solution>

**8. (standard)** Compare GH₵ 10,000 at 20% a year for 10 years under simple and compound interest.

<Solution>

Simple: 10,000 + 10,000 × 0.20 × 10 = **GH₵ 30,000**. Compound: 10,000 × 1.2¹⁰ = 10,000 × 6.1917364 = **GH₵ 61,917.36**. Compounding more than doubles the result.

</Solution>

**9. (standard)** Yaw owes GH₵ 1,500 to a lender charging 3% a month and pays nothing for 6 months. What does he owe?

<Solution>

1,500 × 1.03⁶ = 1,500 × 1.1940523 = **GH₵ 1,791.08** (to the nearest pesewa).

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** You save GH₵ 1,000 at the end of each year at 10% a year. Work out the balance at the end of years 1, 2 and 3. How much of the final balance is interest?

<Solution>

Year 1: 1,000. Year 2: 1,000 × 1.1 + 1,000 = 2,100. Year 3: 2,100 × 1.1 + 1,000 = **3,310**. You paid in 3,000, so **GH₵ 310** is interest.

</Solution>

**11. (stretch)** Two loans of GH₵ 5,000 for one year: Loan A at 30% a year with no fee; Loan B at 28% a year with a GH₵ 200 arrangement fee. Both charge interest once, at the end of the year. Which costs less in total?

<Solution>

A: 0.30 × 5,000 = **GH₵ 1,500**. B: 0.28 × 5,000 + 200 = 1,400 + 200 = **GH₵ 1,600**. Loan A is cheaper, despite the higher interest rate. Its APR is 30%; B’s is 1,600 ÷ 5,000 = 32%.

</Solution>

<Solution>

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Interest** is the payment for using money; the **principal**, **interest rate**, **term** and **balance** describe any loan or deposit.
- **Simple interest** = principal × rate × periods, on the original principal only.
- **Compound interest** adds interest to the balance: principal × (1 + rate)ⁿ. Over decades the difference from simple interest is enormous.
- More frequent compounding raises the **effective annual rate** above the **nominal rate**.
- Compare loans by **APR** (interest plus fees) and savings by **AER** (including compounding).
- The **rule of 72**: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ the yearly rate. It works for debts too.
- Saving regularly means each payment compounds for a different time; a spreadsheet column handles it.
