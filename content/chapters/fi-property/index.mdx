For most families, a home is the largest thing they will ever buy, and land and buildings are often seen as the safest store of wealth there is. Property can be a good investment, but it is also expensive to buy and sell, hard to divide, slow to sell, and usually bought with borrowed money, which magnifies gains and losses alike. By the end of this chapter you will be able to compare the true yearly cost of renting and buying, work out a mortgage payment and its total cost, show how borrowing magnifies a change in price, measure the yield on a property bought to let, and name the ways to invest in property without buying a building. This is general education, not advice about any purchase.

## Owning a home: what you are really buying

A home gives you two things at once: **somewhere to live**, which a tenant also gets for rent, and **an investment** whose value may rise or fall. Comparing renting and buying means comparing the costs of each that you will never get back.

When you rent, the unrecoverable cost is mainly the **rent**.

When you own, the unrecoverable costs are:

- **interest** on the mortgage (the repayment of the loan itself is not a cost; it builds up what you own);
- **maintenance and repairs**, often reckoned at about 1% of the property’s value a year;
- **insurance and property rates**;
- the **opportunity cost** of your deposit: what it could have earned elsewhere (<Ref to="fi-time-value" />);
- **buying and selling costs**: legal fees, agents, taxes, spread over the years you own it.

Against those, the owner gains or loses the change in the property’s price.

## Mortgages

A **mortgage** is a loan to buy property, secured on that property: if you do not repay, the lender can take it and sell it. You pay part of the price yourself, the **deposit**, and borrow the rest. The **loan-to-value ratio** (LTV) is the loan as a share of the price. Repayments are usually fixed monthly amounts over a long **term**, worked out exactly as in <Ref to="fi-loans" />.

**Worked example.** Esi buys a house for GH₵ 600,000 with a 20% deposit of GH₵ 120,000. She borrows GH₵ 480,000 (an LTV of 80%) over 20 years at 18% a year, charged as 1.5% a month.

1. Monthly payment: 480,000 × 0.015 ÷ (1 − 1.015⁻²⁴⁰) = **GH₵ 7,407.90**, or `=PMT(1.5%, 240, -480000)`.
2. Total repaid over 20 years: 7,407.90 × 240 = **GH₵ 1,777,896.00**.
3. Interest: 1,777,896.00 − 480,000 = **GH₵ 1,297,896.00**, nearly three times what she borrowed.

At a rate of 8.4% a year (0.7% a month), the same loan would cost GH₵ 4,135.22 a month. At high interest rates, the cost of borrowing dominates everything else about owning a home.

## Renting against buying, year one

**Worked example.** Esi could instead rent a similar house for GH₵ 3,000 a month, GH₵ 36,000 a year. Her first year of owning costs:

| Cost | GH₵ |
|---|---|
| Mortgage interest in year 1 | 86,183.53 |
| Maintenance (1% of 600,000) | 6,000.00 |
| Insurance and property rates | 2,000.00 |
| Opportunity cost: 120,000 at 9.45% (a 364-day bill) | 11,340.00 |
| **Total** | **105,523.53** |

Of her twelve payments, totalling 88,894.80, only 2,711.27 reduced the loan; the rest was interest. Owning costs 105,523.53 − 36,000 = **GH₵ 69,523.53** more than renting in year one. To come out even, the house would need to rise in value by 69,523.53 ÷ 600,000 = **11.6%** that year, before counting her buying costs.

That does not make renting always better. Over time, the loan shrinks and so does the interest; rent tends to rise with inflation while a fixed mortgage payment does not; owning brings security and freedom to change the home; and in many places prices have risen faster than inflation for long periods. But it shows why “rent is dead money” is too simple: **mortgage interest is dead money too**, and at high rates there is a lot of it.

## Borrowing magnifies gains and losses

Using borrowed money to buy an investment is called **leverage** (or gearing). It means a change in the price produces a much larger change in what you own.

Esi’s **equity** in her home is its value minus what she owes. At the start it is her deposit: 600,000 − 480,000 = 120,000.

- If the price rises 10%, to 660,000, her equity becomes 660,000 − 480,000 = **180,000**: up 50% on her deposit.
- If the price falls 10%, to 540,000, her equity becomes **60,000**: down 50%.
- If the price falls 25%, to 450,000, she owes more than the house is worth: 450,000 − 480,000 = **−30,000**. This is **negative equity**. Selling would not repay the loan.

With an 80% loan, every change in price is multiplied by five in her equity (600,000 ÷ 120,000 = 5).

## Buying to let

**Buy to let** means buying property to rent it out. Its return has two parts: the rent, and the change in price. The **rental yield** measures the first:

- **Gross yield** = yearly rent ÷ price. A house costing 600,000 that rents for 3,000 a month: 36,000 ÷ 600,000 = **6%**.
- **Net yield** subtracts the landlord’s costs: repairs, insurance, rates, agent’s fees, and months with no tenant. If these take a quarter of the rent, net rent is 27,000 and the net yield **4.5%**.

Compare that with the alternatives: a 364-day Treasury bill paid 9.45% in November 2026 with almost no effort. And if the property is bought with a mortgage at 18%, the 4.5% net yield does not come close to covering the interest; the investment only pays if prices rise, which is speculation, not income. Add the work of finding tenants, the risk of damage or unpaid rent, and the fact that a house cannot be sold in a week or in pieces, and buy to let looks far less safe than its reputation.

## Land

In Ghana especially, many people buy **land** and build gradually as money allows. It avoids borrowing, but ties up money for years in something that earns nothing until finished. The biggest risk is the title: land can be sold by someone with no right to sell it, or sold to more than one buyer. Before paying, search the title at the Lands Commission, use a lawyer, and make sure the land is registered in your name.

## Investing in property without buying a building

- **Property funds** hold many buildings or property companies, as in <Ref to="fi-funds" />.
- A **real estate investment trust** (REIT) is a company that owns income-producing property, such as offices, shops or flats, and pays out most of its rental income as dividends. Many are listed on exchanges, so you can buy a small piece of a large, diversified property portfolio and sell it in a day.

These give property’s returns without the concentration, the borrowing or the tenants, though their prices fall when property markets fall, and listed REITs can be as volatile as shares.

<Note>

**Ghana (checked November 2026).** Cedi mortgages have usually carried high interest rates, and many buyers build gradually without one. Before buying land or a house, search the title at the Lands Commission and use a lawyer. Ask any developer for evidence of title and permits, and pay in stages tied to progress, not all at once. Government-backed schemes sometimes offer lower rates; read their terms and eligibility carefully.

</Note>

<Note>

**United Kingdom (checked November 2026).** Mortgages are regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority. Buyers usually pay Stamp Duty Land Tax in England and Northern Ireland, Land and Buildings Transaction Tax in Scotland or Land Transaction Tax in Wales, above certain thresholds, and rental income is taxable. Check GOV.UK for the current thresholds and rates.

</Note>

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing rent with the whole mortgage payment**, instead of rent with interest and the other costs of owning.
- **Forgetting buying and selling costs**, which can take years of price rises to recover.
- **Borrowing the most a lender allows**, leaving no room for a rise in rates or a fall in income.
- **Assuming property prices always rise.** They can fall for years, and leverage turns a fall into a large loss.
- **Counting the gross rental yield** without costs, empty months and tax.
- **Paying for land without checking the title.**

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** A flat costs GH₵ 400,000 and you put down GH₵ 80,000. What is the loan, and the loan-to-value ratio?

<Solution>

Loan = 400,000 − 80,000 = **GH₵ 320,000**. LTV = 320,000 ÷ 400,000 = **80%**.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** A house rents for GH₵ 2,500 a month and costs GH₵ 500,000. What is its gross rental yield?

<Solution>

2,500 × 12 = 30,000 a year. 30,000 ÷ 500,000 = **6%**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Using the flat in problem 1, what happens to the owner’s equity if prices rise 15%? If they fall 15%?

<Solution>

Rise: 400,000 × 1.15 = 460,000; equity = 460,000 − 320,000 = **140,000**, up 75% on the 80,000 deposit. Fall: 340,000 − 320,000 = **20,000**, down 75%. With 80% borrowed, price changes are multiplied by 5.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** In Esi’s mortgage, about how much of her first year’s payments went to interest, and why so much?

<Solution>

86,183.53 of 88,894.80, about **97%**. At 1.5% a month on 480,000, the first month’s interest alone is 7,200, nearly the whole payment of 7,407.90; only as the loan shrinks does more of each payment repay it.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Kojo’s buy-to-let house costs GH₵ 500,000, rents for GH₵ 2,500 a month, and costs him 30% of the rent in repairs, fees and empty months. What is his net yield, and how does it compare with a 364-day bill at 9.45%?

<Solution>

Net rent = 30,000 × 0.70 = 21,000. Net yield = 21,000 ÷ 500,000 = **4.2%**, less than half the bill’s 9.45%. He is relying on the price rising to beat the bill.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Why is a mortgage repayment not entirely a cost of owning, while rent is entirely a cost of renting?

<Solution>

Part of each mortgage payment **repays the loan**, increasing the owner’s equity; that money is still theirs, held in the house. Only the **interest** (plus the other costs of owning) is gone for good. Rent buys the use of the home and nothing else.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Esi’s house falls in value by 25% just after she buys. She loses her job and must sell. What happens?

<Solution>

The house sells for 450,000 (before selling costs) but she owes nearly 480,000. She would lose her whole 120,000 deposit and still owe about **GH₵ 30,000** more, plus selling costs. This is **negative equity**, and it is why borrowing heavily to buy property is risky for anyone whose income is not secure.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Ama has GH₵ 100,000. She could use it as a deposit on a buy-to-let property, or buy units in a listed REIT. Give two advantages of each.

<Solution>

**Buy to let**: she controls the property and can improve it; leverage magnifies gains if prices rise. **REIT**: it is diversified across many properties and tenants; she can sell in a day, invest smaller amounts, and avoids borrowing and the work of being a landlord. (Leverage also magnifies losses, which is a disadvantage of buy to let.)

</Solution>

## Summary

- Owning costs **interest, maintenance, insurance and rates, opportunity cost on the deposit, and buying and selling costs**; compare these with rent, not the whole mortgage payment.
- A **mortgage** is a loan secured on the property; **LTV** is loan ÷ price. At high rates, interest dominates: GH₵ 480,000 over 20 years at 18% costs about GH₵ 1.3 million in interest.
- **Leverage** multiplies price changes in your **equity**; a large fall leads to **negative equity**.
- **Rental yield** = rent ÷ price; judge it net of costs and against safer alternatives.
- Check land and property **titles** before paying.
- **Property funds** and **REITs** give diversified property exposure without borrowing or tenants.
