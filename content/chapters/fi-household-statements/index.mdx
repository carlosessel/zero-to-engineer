Businesses summarise a year in three financial statements, and a household can use the same three to see its year clearly. By the end of this chapter you will be able to draw up a household’s **income statement** (did we live within our income?), **balance sheet** (what do we own and owe, and what is our net worth?) and **cash flow statement** (where did the cash come from and go?), see how the three fit together, and understand why a surplus and a pile of cash are not the same thing. The same ideas, at company scale, come back in the chapters on reading company accounts.

## The household for this chapter

Ama’s year runs from 1 January to 31 December 2026 (fictional, like all the figures). On 1 January she had GH₵ 1,200 in the bank, 300 in her mobile money wallet, a phone worth about 1,500, and a loan of 3,000 still to repay. During the year she earned her salary and some tutoring income, spent as her budget planned, cleared the loan, and in December bought a laptop for GH₵ 4,000 in cash for her tutoring work.

## The income statement: did we live within our means?

An **income statement** covers a period, usually a year or a month. It lists **income** (money earned) and **expenses** (the cost of living in that period), and ends with the difference: a **surplus** if income is larger, a **deficit** if expenses are. (For a business, the same statement is often called the profit and loss account, and the surplus is the profit.)

| Ama’s income statement, 2026 | GH₵ |
|---|---|
| **Income** | |
| Net pay | 48,000 |
| Tutoring | 2,400 |
| **Total income** | **50,400** |
| **Expenses** | |
| Rent | 14,400 |
| Food | 8,400 |
| Transport | 4,560 |
| Electricity and water | 2,160 |
| Phone and data | 1,200 |
| Family support | 3,600 |
| Eating out and entertainment | 3,600 |
| Clothes | 1,080 |
| Interest on the loan | 420 |
| Fall in the phone’s value | 500 |
| **Total expenses** | **39,920** |
| **Surplus** | **10,480** |

Three lines need explaining, because they are where household accounts differ from a list of payments:

- **Only the interest on the loan is an expense.** She paid 3,420 to the lender: 3,000 repaid what she had borrowed (that is not a cost of living; it reduced a debt) and 420 was interest (that is the cost of borrowing).
- **The laptop is not an expense.** Spending 4,000 on it swapped cash for another asset she still owns. It is on the balance sheet, not here.
- **The phone’s fall in value is an expense**, though no money moved. A phone bought for daily use loses value as it ages. Recording that loss each year, here 1,500 to 1,000, is called **depreciation**. Many households skip it for small items; it matters for valuable ones such as a vehicle.

Her **savings rate**, the share of income not spent, is 10,480 ÷ 50,400 = **20.79%**.

## The balance sheet: what we own and owe

A **balance sheet** is a snapshot on one date. It lists **assets** (what you own, at today’s value) and **liabilities** (what you owe), and the difference is your **net worth**, from <Ref to="fi-the-map" />:

net worth = assets − liabilities, or equally, assets = liabilities + net worth

That second way of writing it is why it is called a balance sheet: the two sides always balance.

| Ama’s balance sheet | 1 January 2026 | 31 December 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **Assets** | | |
| Bank current account | 1,200 | 1,180 |
| Savings account | 0 | 4,000 |
| Mobile money | 300 | 300 |
| Phone (current value) | 1,500 | 1,000 |
| Laptop (current value) | 0 | 4,000 |
| **Total assets** | **3,000** | **10,480** |
| **Liabilities** | | |
| Loan | 3,000 | 0 |
| **Total liabilities** | **3,000** | **0** |
| **Net worth** | **0** | **10,480** |

Assets are valued at what they are worth now, not what they cost: a used phone at what it would sell for. Be honest and cautious: an asset you could not sell does not pay a debt. Separate **liquid assets** (cash and savings you can reach quickly) from others, because only liquid assets help in an emergency.

Ama started the year with a net worth of zero and ended it with GH₵ 10,480. Look at the income statement: her surplus was also **10,480**. That is not a coincidence:

change in net worth = surplus (when nothing else changes in value)

A surplus is exactly what makes a household richer over the year, which is why the two statements belong together.

## The cash flow statement: where did the cash go?

Ama had a surplus of 10,480, yet her cash (bank, savings and mobile money) rose by only 5,480 − 1,500 = **3,980**. Where did the rest go? A **cash flow statement** answers that by listing every movement of cash in the period, in three groups:

- **Everyday (operating) cash flow**: cash from income, minus cash paid for expenses.
- **Investing cash flow**: cash spent buying long-lasting assets, or received from selling them.
- **Financing cash flow**: cash from borrowing, minus cash used to repay what was borrowed.

| Ama’s cash flow statement, 2026 | GH₵ |
|---|---|
| Cash received as income | 50,400 |
| Cash paid for expenses (all except the phone’s fall in value) | −39,420 |
| **Everyday cash flow** | **10,980** |
| Bought a laptop | −4,000 |
| **Investing cash flow** | **−4,000** |
| Repaid the loan (the 3,000 borrowed; the interest is above) | −3,000 |
| **Financing cash flow** | **−3,000** |
| **Change in cash** | **3,980** |
| Cash on 1 January | 1,500 |
| **Cash on 31 December** | **5,480** |

Every figure ties to the others: the closing cash of 5,480 is the bank, savings and mobile money on the closing balance sheet (1,180 + 4,000 + 300).

## Surplus is not the same as cash

The three statements show why a household (or a business) can have a good year and still be short of cash, or have plenty of cash and a bad year:

- The 500 fall in the phone’s value lowered the surplus but used no cash.
- The 4,000 laptop used cash but did not lower the surplus: Ama still owns it.
- The 3,000 loan repayment used cash but was not an expense: it reduced a liability.

So her everyday cash flow (10,980) was higher than her surplus (10,480), but her total cash rose much less (3,980), because she chose to use cash to buy an asset and clear a debt. Both were good decisions, and neither shows up as spending on the income statement.

## Useful household measures

From the three statements you can read off a few measures worth tracking each year:

| Measure | How | Ama, end of 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Savings rate | surplus ÷ income | 10,480 ÷ 50,400 = 20.79% |
| Emergency fund in months | liquid assets ÷ monthly essential spending | 5,480 ÷ 3,260 = 1.68 months |
| Debt to assets | liabilities ÷ assets | 0 ÷ 10,480 = 0% |
| Net worth | assets − liabilities | GH₵ 10,480 |

The direction matters more than any one year’s level: a rising net worth and emergency fund, and a falling debt ratio, mean the plan is working.

## Doing it in Python (optional)

If you know some Python, the three statements are a few lines of arithmetic, and the checks that tie them together can be written as assertions that fail if anything is wrong.

```python
income = 48000 + 2400
expenses = 14400 + 8400 + 4560 + 2160 + 1200 + 3600 + 3600 + 1080 + 420 + 500
surplus = income - expenses

everyday = income - (expenses - 500)   # the phone's fall in value used no cash
investing = -4000                      # the laptop
financing = -3000                      # loan repaid
cash_change = everyday + investing + financing

assets_start, liabilities_start = 1200 + 300 + 1500, 3000
assets_end, liabilities_end = 1180 + 4000 + 300 + 1000 + 4000, 0
net_worth_change = (assets_end - liabilities_end) - (assets_start - liabilities_start)

assert net_worth_change == surplus
assert 1500 + cash_change == 1180 + 4000 + 300
print(surplus, everyday, cash_change, net_worth_change)
```

```text
10480 10980 3980 10480
```

## Common mistakes

- **Counting loan repayments as expenses.** Only the interest is a cost of living; the rest reduces a liability.
- **Counting large purchases of lasting things as expenses.** A laptop or vehicle is an asset; its fall in value each year is the expense.
- **Valuing assets at what they cost.** Use what they would sell for today, and be cautious.
- **Forgetting liabilities that are not formal loans**: money owed to family, unpaid bills, a phone instalment plan.
- **Expecting cash to rise by the surplus.** Buying assets and repaying debts use cash without being expenses.
- **Treating one year’s net worth as a verdict.** Track the direction over years.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Kojo’s income for a year was GH₵ 42,000 and his expenses GH₵ 38,500. What was his surplus and his savings rate?

<Solution>

Surplus: 42,000 − 38,500 = **GH₵ 3,500**. Savings rate: 3,500 ÷ 42,000 = **8.33%** (to two decimal places).

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** Esi owns savings of 6,000, a motorbike worth 9,000 and a phone worth 800. She owes 7,500 on the motorbike. What is her net worth?

<Solution>

Assets: 6,000 + 9,000 + 800 = 15,800. Net worth: 15,800 − 7,500 = **GH₵ 8,300**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Yaw paid GH₵ 4,800 to his lender this year: 4,200 repaid what he borrowed and 600 was interest. How much belongs on his income statement as an expense?

<Solution>

Only the interest: **GH₵ 600**. The 4,200 reduced his liability, which shows on the balance sheet and as financing cash flow.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Abena buys a sewing machine for GH₵ 3,000 in cash. What happens to her cash, her net worth and her surplus that day?

<Solution>

Cash falls by 3,000; a new asset worth 3,000 appears, so her **net worth is unchanged**, and her **surplus is unchanged**: buying an asset is not an expense. Later, its fall in value each year will be an expense.

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** A motorbike bought for GH₵ 12,000 is worth 10,200 a year later. What is the depreciation for the year, as an amount and a percentage?

<Solution>

12,000 − 10,200 = **GH₵ 1,800**, which is 1,800 ÷ 12,000 = **15%**.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Fill in the gap: everyday cash flow GH₵ 8,000, investing cash flow −GH₵ 2,500, financing cash flow −GH₵ 1,200. Cash at the start was GH₵ 900. What is cash at the end?

<Solution>

Change in cash: 8,000 − 2,500 − 1,200 = 4,300. End: 900 + 4,300 = **GH₵ 5,200**.

</Solution>

**7. (standard)** Ama’s liquid assets at the end of 2026 were GH₵ 5,480 and her essential spending GH₵ 3,260 a month. How many months of essentials could she cover?

<Solution>

5,480 ÷ 3,260 = **1.68 months** (to two decimal places).

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Kwame had a surplus of GH₵ 6,000 for the year but his cash fell by GH₵ 1,000. Suggest two things that could explain it.

<Solution>

Any two of: he **bought an asset** (a vehicle, equipment, land) with cash; he **repaid borrowing**; he **lent money** to someone (an asset, not an expense); he paid in advance for something next year. Each uses cash without being an expense.

</Solution>

**9. (stretch)** Ama’s net worth rose from 0 to 10,480 and her surplus was 10,480. Next year her surplus is 9,000, but her laptop’s value falls by 1,000 more than she recorded. Assuming nothing else changes, by how much does her net worth rise?

<Solution>

An unrecorded fall in an asset’s value is really an extra expense, so her true surplus is 9,000 − 1,000 = 8,000, and her net worth rises by **GH₵ 8,000**, to 18,480. Valuing assets honestly keeps the two statements in agreement.

</Solution>

**10. (stretch)** Draw up a balance sheet for Akua: savings 2,500; mobile money 400; a fridge worth 1,200; she owes her sister 600 and has an unpaid electricity bill of 150. What are her total assets, liabilities and net worth, and what share of her assets is liquid?

<Solution>

Assets: 2,500 + 400 + 1,200 = **4,100**. Liabilities: 600 + 150 = **750**. Net worth: 4,100 − 750 = **GH₵ 3,350**. Liquid assets (savings and mobile money): 2,900, which is 2,900 ÷ 4,100 = **70.73%** of her assets.

</Solution>

## Summary

- An **income statement** covers a period: **income** − **expenses** = **surplus** (or **deficit**).
- Loan repayments are not expenses (only interest is); buying a lasting asset is not an expense (its yearly **depreciation** is).
- A **balance sheet** is a snapshot: assets = liabilities + net worth. Value assets at today’s worth, and note which are **liquid assets**.
- With nothing else changing, the change in net worth equals the surplus.
- A **cash flow statement** splits cash movements into everyday, investing and financing, and explains the change in cash.
- A surplus is not cash: buying assets and repaying debts use cash without being expenses.
- Track the **savings rate**, emergency months, debt to assets and net worth from year to year.
