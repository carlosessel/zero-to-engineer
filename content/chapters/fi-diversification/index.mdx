“Don’t put all your eggs in one basket” is the oldest advice in investing, and also the most useful. But spreading money around only helps if the pieces behave differently, and choosing the right mix matters more than choosing the right individual investment. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain why diversification reduces risk, what correlation measures, which risks diversification can and cannot remove, how to describe a mix of assets and its expected return, and how to rebalance a portfolio back to its target. As before, this is general education, not personal advice.

## Diversification

**Diversification** means spreading your money across investments that do not all rise and fall together, so that a loss on one is likely to be cushioned by others. Your whole collection of investments is called your **portfolio**.

A street seller who sells both umbrellas and sunglasses has the idea exactly. On rainy days umbrellas sell; on sunny days sunglasses do. Either business alone has good and bad days; together, the takings are steadier.

**Worked example.** Two fictional investments over four years:

| Year | X | Y | Half in each |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +20% | −10% | +5% |
| 2 | −10% | +20% | +5% |
| 3 | +20% | −10% | +5% |
| 4 | −10% | +20% | +5% |

On its own, GH₵ 1,000 in X grows to 1,000 × 1.2 × 0.9 × 1.2 × 0.9 = **GH₵ 1,166.40**, and so does Y. Kept half in each, the portfolio earns 5% every year, with no volatility at all: 1,000 × 1.05⁴ = **GH₵ 1,215.51**. It is both safer and, because it avoids the cost of volatility from <Ref to="fi-risk-return" />, it grows to more.

One condition hides in “kept half in each”: after each year, the investor moves money from the one that rose to the one that fell, to get back to 50:50. That is **rebalancing**, covered below. Without it, the two halves drift apart and the portfolio ends at the same GH₵ 1,166.40 as either investment alone, though with a smoother ride.

Real investments are never this neat. But the principle holds: the less your investments move together, the more diversification helps.

## Correlation

**Correlation** measures how closely two investments move together, on a scale from −1 to +1:

- **+1**: they always move in the same direction, in step. Combining them does not reduce risk.
- **0**: no relationship. Combining them reduces risk.
- **−1**: they always move in opposite directions, as X and Y do. Combining them can remove volatility entirely.

In a spreadsheet, `=CORREL(B2:B5, C2:C5)` gives the correlation of two columns of returns; for X and Y it gives −1. Most real investments have correlations between 0 and +1: shares in two banks in the same country are highly correlated; shares and government bonds are often less so. Correlations can also change, and often rise in a crisis, when many prices fall together just when you most want them not to.

## What diversification can and cannot remove

Risk comes in two kinds:

- **Specific risk** belongs to one company, building or scheme: a factory fire, a fraud, a bad manager, a tenant who stops paying. Holding many different investments makes any one of these matter little. This risk can be **diversified away**.
- **Market risk** (also called systematic risk) affects nearly everything at once: a recession, a currency crisis, a sharp rise in interest rates. No number of shares in the same market removes it.

Because specific risk can be removed cheaply, by holding many investments, markets do not reward you for bearing it. Owning one company’s shares carries more risk than owning a hundred companies’ shares, but no more expected return. That is why concentration is usually a bad bet.

Common forms of **concentration** to watch for:

- most of your savings in your employer’s shares, so a single bad event could cost you your job and your savings at once;
- everything in one property, one plot of land or one business;
- all your savings with one institution, above the deposit protection limit (<Ref to="fi-banking" />);
- everything in one currency, when your future costs (school fees abroad, imported goods) are in another.

## Asset allocation

An **asset class** is a broad group of investments that behave similarly: cash, bonds, shares, property, and others such as commodities. **Asset allocation** is how you divide your portfolio among asset classes, and it explains most of the ups and downs a diversified investor experiences, far more than which individual shares or bonds they pick.

A portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of its parts.

**Worked example.** Esi puts 30% of her savings in Treasury bills paying about 9% a year and 70% in a share fund she expects, on average, to return 16% a year (an assumption, not a promise). Expected return: 0.30 × 9% + 0.70 × 16% = 2.7% + 11.2% = **13.9%** a year. Volatility does not average in the same way: because the two parts are not perfectly correlated, the mix is less volatile than its parts would suggest.

How to choose a mix depends on the previous chapter:

- **Time horizon**: money needed soon in cash and short-term bills; long-term money can hold more shares.
- **Risk capacity and tolerance**: take less risk if a fall would hurt your life or make you panic and sell.
- **Goals**: an income in retirement suggests more bonds; growth over decades suggests more shares.
- **Currency**: if your future costs are in cedis, most of your investments should be too, though some foreign holdings diversify against a fall in the cedi.

There is no single right mix. A common rule of thumb, that the share of bonds should roughly equal your age, is only a starting point for discussion; it ignores your other assets, income and plans.

## Rebalancing

As prices move, your portfolio drifts away from the mix you chose. **Rebalancing** means selling some of what has grown and buying what has shrunk, to return to your target. It keeps your risk where you intended, and it makes you sell high and buy low by rule, rather than by feeling.

**Worked example.** Kojo’s target is 60% shares and 40% bonds. He starts with GH₵ 10,000: 6,000 in shares and 4,000 in bonds. A year later shares have risen 25% and bonds 5%:

1. Shares: 6,000 × 1.25 = 7,500. Bonds: 4,000 × 1.05 = 4,200. Total: **11,700**.
2. Shares are now 7,500 ÷ 11,700 = **64.1%** of the portfolio: riskier than he chose.
3. His target for shares is 60% of 11,700 = 7,020, and for bonds 40% = 4,680.
4. He sells 7,500 − 7,020 = **GH₵ 480** of shares and buys GH₵ 480 of bonds.

Two common ways to rebalance: on a **calendar** (once a year), or when an asset class drifts more than a set amount from its target (say 5 percentage points). Each sale may have costs and tax, so rebalancing too often wastes money. Adding new savings to whichever part is under its target rebalances without selling at all.

## Common mistakes

- **Owning many investments that all move together**, such as five funds holding the same shares, and calling it diversified.
- **Concentrating in what you know**: your employer, your town’s property, one sector.
- **Diversifying into things you do not understand**, just to have more of them.
- **Never rebalancing**, so a portfolio built as 60% shares drifts to 80% after a long rise, just before a fall.
- **Abandoning the plan after a fall**, selling the part that fell instead of rebalancing into it.
- **Expecting diversification to prevent all losses.** Market risk remains.

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Which is more diversified: shares in three Ghanaian banks, or shares in a bank, a telecoms company and a food producer? Why?

<Solution>

The **mix of three sectors**. Three banks are exposed to the same risks (interest rates, bad loans, banking rules), so they are highly correlated. Different sectors are affected by different events.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** A portfolio is 50% in an investment expected to return 10% and 50% in one expected to return 20%. What is its expected return?

<Solution>

0.5 × 10% + 0.5 × 20% = **15%**.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** Ama’s portfolio is 20% Treasury bills (expected 9%), 30% bonds (expected 12%) and 50% shares (expected 17%). What is its expected return?

<Solution>

0.2 × 9 + 0.3 × 12 + 0.5 × 17 = 1.8 + 3.6 + 8.5 = **13.9%**.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** A 50:50 portfolio of GH₵ 20,000 has shares that fell 20% and bonds that rose 10%. What are the values now, and what trades restore 50:50?

<Solution>

Shares 10,000 × 0.80 = 8,000; bonds 10,000 × 1.10 = 11,000; total 19,000. Target for each: 9,500. **Sell GH₵ 1,500 of bonds and buy GH₵ 1,500 of shares.**

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Kwame works for a listed company and holds 80% of his savings in its shares. Describe the risk and one way to reduce it.

<Solution>

**Concentration**: if the company struggles, its share price may fall at the same time as his job is at risk, so his income and savings suffer together. He could sell some each year and move the money into a diversified fund, cash or bonds.

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Why don’t markets pay extra expected return for owning a single company instead of many?

<Solution>

Its extra risk is **specific risk**, which anyone can remove cheaply by diversifying. Investors who diversify will happily pay a price that does not reward that risk, so the concentrated investor takes more risk for no more expected return.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** In the X and Y example, what would the 50:50 portfolio be worth after four years without rebalancing? Explain why it differs from the rebalanced one.

<Solution>

Each half grows like its investment: 500 × 1.1664 + 500 × 1.1664 = **GH₵ 1,166.40**. Without rebalancing, the portfolio drifts towards whichever did well, so its yearly return is no longer a steady 5%, and the volatility cost returns. Rebalancing kept it at exactly 50:50 and gave **GH₵ 1,215.51**.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Esi’s target is 70% shares, 30% bonds, and she rebalances when either is more than 5 percentage points away. Her GH₵ 50,000 has become 39,000 in shares and 14,000 in bonds. Should she rebalance? If so, how?

<Solution>

Total 53,000; shares are 39,000 ÷ 53,000 = 73.6%, only 3.6 points over target. **No rebalancing needed yet.** If she is adding new savings, she could put them into bonds to move back towards 70:30 without selling.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Diversification** spreads money across investments that don’t move together, so losses on some are cushioned by others.
- **Correlation** (−1 to +1) measures how closely two investments move; the lower it is, the more diversification helps.
- **Specific risk** can be diversified away and is not rewarded; **market risk** cannot.
- Avoid **concentration** in one company, employer, property, institution or currency.
- **Asset allocation**, the split between cash, bonds, shares and property, drives most of a portfolio’s behaviour; its expected return is the weighted average.
- **Rebalancing** restores the target mix by selling what grew and buying what shrank, on a calendar or when drift passes a limit.
