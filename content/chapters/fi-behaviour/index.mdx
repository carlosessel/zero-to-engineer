Most of the damage people do to their money comes not from not knowing the arithmetic, but from how human minds react to gains, losses, other people and uncertainty. These reactions are normal, predictable and costly. By the end of this chapter you will be able to name the most common behavioural mistakes in money (loss aversion, chasing past returns, overconfidence, herding, market timing and mental accounting), see what each one costs in numbers, and set up simple rules that protect you from yourself. This is general education, not advice.

## Why we make predictable mistakes

**Behavioural finance** studies how people actually make money decisions, as opposed to how a perfectly logical person would. Our instincts evolved for a world of immediate dangers, not for decades of saving and volatile markets. Fear and excitement, the urge to follow the crowd, and the comfort of a good story all push us towards decisions that feel right at the time and look foolish later.

Knowing about these biases helps only a little; the most effective protection is to **decide in advance**, in calm conditions, what you will do, and to make the right action automatic.

## Loss aversion

**Loss aversion** is feeling a loss much more strongly than a gain of the same size: losing GH₵ 1,000 hurts more than winning GH₵ 1,000 pleases. Studies have often found losses weigh roughly twice as heavily. It leads to:

- **Refusing sensible risk**, such as keeping long-term savings in cash because shares might fall, and losing to inflation instead (<Ref to="fi-inflation" />).
- **Selling in a panic** after a fall, turning a temporary loss on paper into a permanent one.
- **Holding losers and selling winners**: keeping a share that has fallen “until it gets back to what I paid”, while selling one that has risen to “lock in the gain”. What you paid is irrelevant to what a share will do next.

**What to do instead**: judge every holding by the question “would I buy it today at this price?”, and set your asset allocation (<Ref to="fi-diversification" />) so that a normal fall is one you could sit through.

## Chasing past returns

**Recency bias** is giving too much weight to what happened recently. In investing it shows up as **chasing returns**: buying whatever did best last year, and selling whatever did worst.

**Worked example.** A fund returns +30%, +25%, −30%, +20% and +15% over five years.

- **Esi** invests GH₵ 10,000 at the start and holds: 10,000 × 1.30 × 1.25 × 0.70 × 1.20 × 1.15 = **GH₵ 15,697.50**, about 9.4% a year.
- **Kojo** keeps his GH₵ 10,000 as cash until he has seen two great years, then invests at the start of year 3. The fund falls 30%, so he sells in fear and holds cash at 5% through year 4, missing the recovery. Seeing the fund rise, he buys back for year 5: 10,000 × 0.70 × 1.05 × 1.15 = **GH₵ 8,452.50**.

Same fund, same five years. Esi gained 57%; Kojo lost 15%. The gap between what a fund earns and what its investors earn, because of when they buy and sell, is often called the **behaviour gap**.

<Walkthrough name="fi-behaviour-chasing" />

**What to do instead**: invest regularly, by standing order, regardless of the news, and never choose an investment because of last year’s return alone.

## Overconfidence

**Overconfidence** is believing you know more, or can predict better, than you can. Most people rate themselves above-average drivers, and above-average investors. It leads to **trading too much**, **concentrating** in a few “sure things”, and **ignoring evidence**, such as the SPIVA results in <Ref to="fi-funds" /> that most professionals fail to beat the index.

Trading costs add up quickly: someone who makes ten round trips a year, each costing 1% in commission and spread, loses 1 − 0.99¹⁰ = **9.6%** of their money a year to costs before any gain or loss.

**What to do instead**: write down why you bought each investment and what would prove you wrong; check later how often you were right; and keep any “stock picking” to a small part of your money.

## Herding and hype

**Herding** is doing what others are doing because they are doing it. It is how bubbles form: rising prices attract buyers, which raises prices, until there are no buyers left. Social media makes it faster: in 2021, an FCA survey of under-40s who invest in high-risk products such as crypto and foreign exchange trading found that 58% said hype on social media and in the news lay behind their decisions, and 76% said they felt competitive with friends, family and their own past investments.

**What to do instead**: when everyone is talking about an investment, especially people who have never invested before, take it as a warning, not an invitation. Ask what the investment produces (<Ref to="fi-valuation" />), not how fast its price has risen.

## Market timing

**Market timing** is trying to sell before falls and buy before rises. It requires being right twice: when to get out, and when to get back in. In practice, the best and worst days in markets tend to come close together, during periods of panic, so investors who sell after a fall often miss the sharp rebound, as Kojo did.

**What to do instead**: decide your asset allocation, invest regularly, and rebalance on a schedule (<Ref to="fi-diversification" />). Rebalancing makes you sell some of what rose and buy some of what fell, by rule rather than by feeling.

## Mental accounting and other traps

- **Mental accounting**: treating money differently depending on which “pot” it is in. Keeping GH₵ 5,000 in savings at 10% a year while owing GH₵ 5,000 on a card at 4% a month (60.1% a year) costs about 50% a year on that money. Money is money; pay the expensive debt (<Ref to="fi-debt" />).
- **Anchoring**: fixing on a number, such as the price you paid or a past high, that has nothing to do with value now.
- **Present bias**: valuing today far above tomorrow, so retirement saving never starts (<Ref to="fi-retirement" />).
- **Confirmation bias**: noticing only the information that supports what you already believe.
- **The sunk cost fallacy**: carrying on with a bad investment or project because of what you have already put into it.

## Rules that protect you

- **Automate** saving and investing, on payday, so willpower is not needed.
- **Write an investment plan**: your allocation, what you buy, how often you rebalance, and what you will do in a 30% fall. Read it when markets panic.
- **Use a waiting period**: for any investment decision prompted by news or excitement, wait 48 hours.
- **Look less often.** Checking long-term investments daily makes losses feel more frequent and tempts you to act.
- **Keep an emergency fund**, so you never have to sell investments at a bad time.

## Common mistakes

- **Selling after a fall** and buying back after a rise.
- **Choosing investments by recent returns.**
- **Trading often**, paying costs and taxes each time.
- **Following tips** from social media, friends or “mentors”.
- **Holding a loser to get back to the price you paid.**
- **Keeping savings while carrying expensive debt.**

## Practice

**1. (warm-up)** Ama bought a share at GH₵ 5.00. It is now GH₵ 3.00. She says she will sell “when it gets back to 5”. Which bias is this?

<Solution>

**Anchoring** on the purchase price, linked to **loss aversion**. The right question is whether she would buy it today at GH₵ 3.00.

</Solution>

**2. (warm-up)** What does trading eight round trips a year cost, if each costs 1.5% in fees and spread?

<Solution>

1 − 0.985⁸ = **11.4%** of the money a year.

</Solution>

**3. (standard)** In the worked example, what would Kojo have had if, after investing at the start of year 3, he had simply held to the end of year 5?

<Solution>

10,000 × 0.70 × 1.20 × 1.15 = **GH₵ 9,660**. Still a loss, because he bought at the top after two great years, but GH₵ 1,207.50 more than he got by selling and buying back.

</Solution>

**4. (standard)** Kwame has GH₵ 6,000 in a savings account at 9% a year and owes GH₵ 6,000 on a loan at 3% a month. Roughly what does keeping both cost him a year?

<Solution>

The loan costs 1.03¹² − 1 = 42.6% a year, about GH₵ 2,555; the savings earn 9%, GH₵ 540. Keeping both costs about **GH₵ 2,000 a year**. (He should keep a small emergency fund and use the rest to pay down the loan.)

</Solution>

**5. (standard)** Your friends are all buying a new token that has risen 400% in a month. Name the biases at work and two questions to ask.

<Solution>

**Herding**, **recency bias**, and fear of missing out. Ask: **what does it produce**, and so what is it worth? And **who is selling** to all these new buyers, and why?

</Solution>

**6. (standard)** Write one rule that would have stopped Kojo’s mistakes.

<Solution>

For example: “I invest GH₵ X every month by standing order into a diversified fund and do not sell in a fall unless my plan says to.” Any rule that is **automatic** and **written in advance** would have kept him invested through year 4.

</Solution>

**7. (stretch)** Why might checking your investments every day make you more likely to sell at a bad time?

<Solution>

Over a single day, a share fund falls almost as often as it rises, so daily checking shows frequent losses. Because of **loss aversion**, each one hurts more than a gain pleases, so the investment feels worse than its long-run record. That discomfort tempts you to act. Checking less often shows mostly the long-term trend.

</Solution>

**8. (stretch)** Esi’s investment plan says she will rebalance to 60% shares and 40% bonds each January. Shares have just fallen 30%. Explain why following her plan is likely to be better than following her feelings.

<Solution>

Her feelings (loss aversion, herding) will urge her to sell shares. Her plan makes her **buy** shares, at lower prices, to restore 60%. Because she wrote the plan in calm conditions and with long-term reasoning, it is more likely to serve her goals than a decision made in fear, and it keeps her invested for the recovery that panicked sellers often miss.

</Solution>

## Summary

- **Behavioural finance** studies predictable money mistakes; the best defence is deciding in advance and automating.
- **Loss aversion**: losses hurt about twice as much; it leads to panic selling and holding losers.
- **Chasing returns** (recency bias) creates a **behaviour gap**: Esi gained 57% holding; Kojo lost 15% in the same fund.
- **Overconfidence** leads to too much trading and concentration; costs mount quickly.
- **Herding** and hype drive bubbles; social media speeds them up.
- **Market timing** requires being right twice; rebalancing by rule is better.
- Watch for **mental accounting**, **anchoring**, **present bias**, **confirmation bias** and **sunk costs**.
